# THE WILL

by Nell Harrow

**PROLOGUE**

Nobody can ever know I was here.

That is the first clear thought I have had since I walked into this field, and it is a terrible thing to think, because my father is lying ten feet away from me under the back wheel of his tractor.

The engine is still running. It shakes the whole machine, and the machine shakes the fence it has rolled into, and the sound goes out over the river as if nothing has happened. As if he has only climbed down for a minute.

I can see one of his hands. Palm up in the mud, open. In eighty-one years he never once opened it for any of us. I can see his green cap in the furrow. He has worn it since before I could read. I am not going to look at the rest of him again. I looked once.

"Dad."

It comes out of me like a cough. Nothing moves except the machine.

"Dad. Get up."

He does not get up. I should be on my knees in the mud. I should be running for the house, screaming for help. Instead I am standing here with my hands jammed into my pockets so that I cannot see them shake, and what I feel, God help me, is relief.

He frightened me every day of my life. This frightens me more.

He would hate this. Not being dead. The diesel. He counted every drop that went into that tank, and now it is burning for nothing. I could reach up and turn the key. Two seconds. I cannot make myself touch it.

Everybody told him this machine would kill him. The brake has been gone for twenty years. When the safety switch broke, he twisted the wires together rather than pay forty dollars for a new one, and from that day you could start her from the ground with her in gear, and she would walk straight over whoever was standing beside her. He told everybody to mind their own business.

Forty dollars.

He had only just started. There is one black strip of plowed earth behind the machine, and the rest of the field has not been touched.

*You'll get this land over my dead body.* He said it to all of us, every time one of us asked, for years. Well. There it is.

And last night I told him what I would do on his grave, and half the county heard me. I can see every face in that room. They will remember every word.

The house is right there on the rise, and the kitchen window looks straight down into this field. From up there you can see the shape of things: the tractor, the fence, a person standing beside them. You cannot see a face.

Then the screen door bangs, and a voice comes across the field, thin and old.

"Mr. Hale? Mr. Hale, is that you?"

She cannot see the ground from there. She can see the tractor, and someone standing beside it, and to her whoever stands beside his tractor is him.

I don't answer. I don't move. She waits. In forty years he never answered her, and she is used to it. The door bangs again, and she has gone back in.

I don't touch him. I don't touch the tractor. The car is on the lane by the gate, on gravel. Gravel does not hold a tire track. But the ground here is soft, and I can see every step I took coming in, one line of them across the wet earth. One line is better than two. I know that is not true. I do it anyway.

I leave the engine running. I walk back to the car in my own footprints.


**PART ONE: NORA**

**CHAPTER 1**

*Four weeks later*

"Nobody goes to Marlow on purpose," says the man in the seat beside me. "So what did you do?"

It's a joke. I know it's a joke. He's grinning, and men on buses make jokes.

It still takes me a second.

"I got a letter," I say.

His name is Sam. He got on in Indianapolis four hours ago with a duffel bag and a paper cup of coffee, and he asked if the seat was taken. That is the only question of his I have answered in full. He's about thirty-five and good-looking, and he found that out early. His jacket cost real money about five winters ago. On his left wrist there's a pale stripe where a watch used to be. A wide one.

He's also wearing cologne. I know which one. I once knew a man who wore it, and that's as far as I let that thought go.

I've handled men like Sam six days a week for years. You smile, you keep moving, you never sit down. The trouble with a bus is that you're already sitting down.

"A letter from who?"

"A lawyer."

"That's never good."

"This one says it is."

The envelope is in my bag, under my hand. I've read what's inside it so many times that the folds have gone soft. It says a man has died. It says a name is in his will, and it's the name on the envelope. I'm asked to come to an office in Marlow, Indiana, at ten o'clock tomorrow morning. A bus ticket was clipped to the page. A room has been reserved.

It doesn't say how much.

A lawyer's letter usually tells you what you're getting. This one tells me where to sit and what time.

I've decided on a few thousand dollars. I picked that number because it's the biggest one I can think about without feeling stupid. A few thousand dollars is three weeks of back rent and one whole month of not counting.

I had a roommate once. She died, and I found out what an apartment costs when you divide it by one. I never looked for another roommate. I'm not easy to live with, and there's nobody I'd trust with a key. Now I rent a room over a dry cleaner's, and I'm behind on that too.

So I want this money. I want it enough that I traded two shifts to Denise, who will want them back with interest. That's a hundred and sixty dollars in tips I won't see. I told my boss there had been a death, which is true, and I let him assume the rest.

And here is what I know about free money. There isn't any. When somebody hands you something for nothing, there's a piece of paper somewhere with your name on it, and that paper is the bill.

"You think it's a scam," Sam says.

"I think a stranger mailed me a bus ticket."

"A scam asks you for money. Did he ask you for money?"

"Not yet."

He laughs. Outside, the land is the flattest I've ever seen, cut down to stubble, with the sun low and orange on it. Out here you could watch a person coming for an hour.

"What would you do?" I ask.

I don't know why I ask. It's a long time since I asked anybody that.

He actually thinks about it. "Free ride, free bed? I'd go. I'd listen. And I wouldn't sign a thing until I'd read it twice."

"I read everything twice."

"Everything?"

"Everything."

I learned that the expensive way.

"Which lawyer?" he says, and then he answers himself. "Doesn't matter. There's only one in Marlow. Everett Lang. He did my grandfather's will. He read us every word of it out loud, commas and all. Bring a sandwich."

That's the name on the letterhead. I looked it up at the library before I gave a day of my life to a bus. The lawyer is real. He has had the same office for thirty-four years. I looked up the dead man too. He got four lines in a county newspaper, as if somebody was paying by the word. A farmer, eighty-one, died at his farm. Three children: Wade, Colleen, Theodore. I read the names twice, and I didn't know any of them.

What I couldn't look up is why. I've built a dozen stories to explain it since Milwaukee, and not one of them has lasted a mile. The only one I can stand is that somebody made a mistake.

"Your turn," I say. "What did you do?"

"To end up in Marlow? I was born near it." He stretches his legs into the aisle. "I'm going to my grandmother's. You get off in Marlow because it's the only stop there is. Then it's twenty-five minutes of county road, straight at the river. Her place, then one more farm at the end of a lane, then water."

"How do you get out there?"

"She picks me up. She's eighty-nine and she drives like somebody's chasing her."

"You don't have a car?" I ask it gently. I had a bus ticket mailed to me. I don't know what his excuse is.

"It's in the shop."

Everybody's car is in the shop. In all my years of carrying plates I have never met a man whose car was repossessed. I look at the pale stripe on his wrist. I'd say the watch lasted about a month longer than the car.

"You visit her a lot?"

"Every three months." He says it lightly. "She's got a hundred and sixty acres on the river. For fifty years it was worth what corn is worth."

"And now?"

"Now a company wants to build one of those data centers out there, and Grandma is sitting on a number. She won't tell me the number. She tells the cat."

"So you visit."

"So I visit. I fix what's broken. I eat what she cooks. I let her win at gin."

Four bus tickets a year to stay in an old woman's will. As investments go, I've seen worse.

Besides, I'm on a bus going toward a dead man's money myself. I'm in no position to judge him.

"What do you do up in Milwaukee?" he asks.

"I wait tables."

It's true, and it's all of the truth that anyone gets. There was a time when I was paid to do exactly the kind of arithmetic I just did on him. I don't mention it.

"So has she sold?" I ask.

"She can't." It's the first time he has stopped smiling. "They want the whole bend of the river or nothing. Her farm and the big one next door. She's eighty-nine, and the old man next door wouldn't sell them a fence post."

"Wouldn't?"

"He died last month. She called me Sunday. She doesn't want to be alone out there right now."

I know which name is coming.

"Amos Hale. Tractor went over him. Eighty-one and still plowing his own fields, so I don't know what anybody expected. Meanest man in three counties. He and Grandma couldn't stand each other for sixty years, and now she won't sleep with the porch light off." He shakes his head. "She's got theories. She's got theories about the mailman, too."

The newspaper said *at his farm*. It didn't say under what.

I keep my face still. I'm good at it. You learn it waiting tables. You learn it other places too.

But Sam is quicker than he acts. I watch him get there.

"Hang on." He turns in his seat. "One lawyer in Marlow. One funeral this fall that was worth a lawyer. Who's your dead man, Nora?"

I could lie. But at ten tomorrow I walk into that lawyer's office, in a town with one lawyer and one dead farmer. By noon everybody will know. The lie would last eighteen hours.

"Amos Hale," I say.

He whistles. "You knew Amos?"

"No."

"You're family?"

"I never heard his name until the letter came."

That's the truth. It may be the truest thing I've told anyone in a long while, and I watch him put it in his pocket. His grandmother will have it tonight. After her, I can't guess.

"So what did he leave you?"

"It doesn't say."

"Ballpark."

"It doesn't say."

"You must have done *something* for him. A man like that doesn't just—" He stops, and he looks at me. It's quick. The hair, the coat, the rest.

I know that look. He's adding me up.

I know what he sees, too, because I picked it out myself. The coat is camel wool from a church thrift shop, one size too big. I bought it for tomorrow. People are slower to cheat a woman who looks like she doesn't need the money. The hair is blonde out of a box, done over a sink, with an inch of dark at the roots that I meant to fix before this trip. The box costs eleven dollars. The rest of it I can't do anything about. It has been worth about four dollars a table to me, and it has cost me more than that.

And I know the answer he gets. A rich old man, a younger woman nobody has heard of, a will. It's the wrong answer. I don't correct it.

Marlow, when it comes, is a water tower, then a grain elevator, then a gas station. The bus pulls in beside the pumps. The sun is almost down.

Sam is already on his feet, pulling his duffel down from the rack. "This is us, Nora."

I don't move.

"Nora?"

"Sorry," I say. "Long day."

Outside it's cold, and it smells of diesel and cut fields. The driver hauls my suitcase out from under the bus. It's small and navy blue, and the zipper pull broke off years ago. I packed it for two nights.

A big old Buick is waiting by the air hose with its engine running. The window comes down. Behind the wheel is a woman so small that I can see her only from the nose up. She doesn't look at her grandson. She looks at me, and she goes on looking for longer than is polite.

I get that look from women her age. It usually means: stay away from my grandson.

"We'll run you to the motel," Sam says.

"It's right there." It is: a low building across the road, twelve doors in a row and a lit office at one end.

"Then let me walk you into Lang's tomorrow. I know him. I know all of them. You don't want to walk into that room cold."

"I've been walking into rooms by myself for a long time."

He writes a phone number on the back of his bus ticket anyway. "Tanner. We're the only ones in the book." I take it, because taking it is quicker than not taking it.

He gets into the Buick. He's talking before the door is shut. The old woman still hasn't looked at him. Then she pulls out onto the road without slowing down for it, and they're gone.

The office of the Marlow Motor Inn has a bell on the counter and a man of about sixty behind it, with reading glasses pushed down his nose. He's doing a crossword in pen, which tells me most of what I need to know about him. He doesn't look up.

"There should be a room for Bell," I say.

He finds a card in a box. He asks for ID, and I give him my license. He copies down the number and turns the card around for me to sign.

I read it first. The front, and then the back. He sighs through his nose. He can sigh all he wants.

Near the bottom, somebody has typed: *Bill to: Estate of Amos Hale, c/o E. Lang, Attorney.*

So a dead man is paying for my bed. That's a first.

I sign. Whenever I sign something in front of another person, I wait for them to look up. Nobody ever does. I watch for it anyway.

This time, somebody looks up.

Not at the signature. He's reading the line at the bottom. He takes off his glasses and looks at me properly, the hair and the coat and the suitcase, the way Sam did, only slower.

"Hale," he says. "So you're the one."

"The one what?"

He takes a key off the board behind him and puts it on the counter. He doesn't push it toward me. I have to reach.

"Room nine."

He goes back to his crossword. I stand there long enough to see that the pen isn't moving.


**CHAPTER 2**

The talking stops when I walk in.

Not all at once. It goes table by table, front to back, and by the time the door shuts behind me the only sound in the place is something frying.

I'm used to being looked at. I've had it since I was fourteen, and I know every kind there is. This isn't one of them. Nobody smiles. Nobody looks away when I look back.

I could turn around. But I haven't eaten since a pack of crackers in Indianapolis, and I've already tried everywhere else. I put my suitcase on the bed in room nine and walked straight out again. I couldn't tell you what color the walls are. Main Street took me four minutes, end to end. There's one grocery, and it closes at six. There's a tavern with a beer sign in the window, and I don't walk into bars alone in towns I don't know. And there's this: a diner called Lottie's, with steamed-up windows and a full room.

So I walk to the counter as if thirty people aren't watching me do it.

The menu is on the wall in white plastic letters. I read all of it, because I read everything. Then I read the right-hand side again, the side with the numbers. The Estate of Amos Hale is paying for my bed. It isn't paying for my supper. I have forty-one dollars: a twenty, a ten, a five and six ones. I counted them on the bus and again in room nine. It's still forty-one.

What I want is the hot roast beef plate at $11.95. I want it the way I've wanted very few things.

"A grilled cheese and a cup of the soup, please," I say. "And water. From the tap is fine."

That's seven dollars. The woman behind the counter writes it down without a word. She's about fifty, with a pencil behind her ear and a name tag that says PAT. So she isn't Lottie, or she isn't admitting it. She's working a full room alone. I've been her. Any other night, I'd feel sorry for her.

"Sit anywhere," she says, to the pad.

Anywhere is a problem. There isn't an empty table in the place. I stand there looking for one, and that's when I see something odd. At half of these tables, the people aren't together. A man in a seed cap is eating pie beside a woman with a church bulletin, and neither one has looked at the other. Marlow has more people than chairs. You sit where there's a chair.

There's a chair at a table for four by the window, where two men in their sixties are working through the meatloaf. I don't know the rule. Do I ask? Do I say good evening? I sit down and say nothing. Neither do they. One of them moves the ketchup an inch toward me. It's either a welcome or a border.

Little by little, the talking starts again. But every time I lift my head, somebody's eyes are just leaving me.

What is wrong with this place?

I tell myself it's the size of it. A town like this gets maybe one new face a year, and tonight I'm it.

But that isn't how they're looking. A new face gets *who's that?* What I'm getting is *that's her*. I've had it once tonight already, from a man with a crossword.

"Hello, stranger."

He's standing behind the last empty chair with a coffee cup in his hand. He's in his late thirties and good-looking, in a quiet way, as if it isn't news to him and doesn't much interest him. His coat is the kind the farmers in here are wearing, dark green canvas, except his still has the store creases in the sleeves. His boots have never met a field.

Whatever he is, he isn't a farmer.

"Don't take it personally," he says. "I'm not from here either. I've been eating at that counter on and off for a year, and they still watch me chew. May I?"

Twice in one day a good-looking man has found me already sitting down. I should start eating on my feet.

But in a room like this, one person who'll talk to me is worth having. I nod at the chair.

He sits. One of the old men looks at him, then at his friend, and they both go back to the meatloaf.

I try to do the arithmetic on him. New coat. Clean boots. A watch, still on his wrist. No ring, and no pale stripe where a ring used to be. I always check.

It doesn't add up to anything. In my experience, the men I can't add up are the expensive ones.

"Around my fourth visit, somebody nodded at me," he says. "I nearly wrote home about it."

I laugh. It's out before I can stop it. It's been a while, and it sounds like somebody else's.

"There," he says. "Now they'll really stare. Between us, this table holds the two least welcome people in the county."

That's a lot to say about a woman who got off a bus an hour ago. I let it go. It's a figure of speech.

Pat brings my plate. She puts it down without looking at him once.

"Are they always like this?" I ask.

"They've been worse lately."

"Why lately?"

He turns his cup around on the table. "A man died out by the river about a month ago. They found him under his own tractor."

"Here?"

"Half an hour out. But the only road to his place runs past that window, so the town counts him as its own." He shakes his head. "I'm sorry. That's no talk for somebody's supper."

I should leave it there. But a woman with no letter in her bag would be curious. So I am.

"Who was he?"

"A farmer named Hale. Eighty-one. He had the most land in the county and the fewest friends."

"A tractor sounds like an accident," I say.

"That's what the sheriff wrote down." He looks around the room. "Nobody in here believes it. They're locking doors that haven't seen a key in fifty years."

Somewhere out by the river, an old woman is sleeping with her porch light on. I don't mention her.

I came here hoping for a few thousand dollars from a man who died. Nobody said anything about a man who was killed.

"What do you think?" I ask.

"I think they're scared of the wrong thing." He doesn't lower his voice. One of the old men stops chewing. "Nobody is coming through their windows. If somebody put that old man under his tractor, it was somebody who wanted him gone. Him, and nobody else."

"Who would want that?"

"Out here?" He smiles into his coffee. "Who wouldn't? His own children could hardly stand him."

He says it the way other people talk about the weather. I think it's meant as a comfort.

It would be, if I didn't have the dead man's name in my bag. I look at the room again. I'm the stranger who came in on the bus to collect from him. Tonight, to these people, I might look like somebody who wanted him gone.

"How do you know all this?"

"Nobody knows where anything comes from in this town. You sit still long enough and it lands on you."

"You said you weren't from here."

He smiles as if I've caught him at cards. "I'm not. I just keep finding myself here."

"Family?"

"Business."

One word, and then he drinks his coffee. I know a closed door when I see one. I have a few of my own, and I like a man who doesn't ask me to open them.

That's when I notice. He hasn't asked me one thing. Not where I'm from, not what I'm doing here, not even my name. Sam got my job, my city and my dead man out of me in four hours. This man has asked me whether a chair was free.

It's the easiest company I've had all day.

He finishes his coffee and stands up. "Eat that while it's hot. It's the best thing on that wall, and I've tried the wall."

At the register he says something to Pat that I can't hear. On his way to the door, the man in the seed cap has his chair pushed out into the aisle. He doesn't pull it in. The stranger waits a second, still smiling, and then walks around him.

The bell over the door rings, and he's gone.

I eat every crumb. I learned a long time ago to eat when there's food, whatever else is going on.

Then I go up to the register and count out a five and three ones. Seven for the food, and one for Pat, because I know what a dollar is on a night like this.

She pushes it back.

"You're paid."

"I haven't paid."

"He did." She nods at the door.

A free supper. I know what I think about free.

I take back seven dollars and leave the eighth where it is.

"Who is he?"

She looks at me to see if I'm joking. "You sat with him half an hour."

"He didn't say."

"No. I don't guess he would." She takes the pencil from behind her ear and puts it back. "That's the data center man."

*They want the whole bend of the river or nothing. The old man next door wouldn't sell them a fence post.* I heard it on a bus this afternoon.

"He told me he had business here."

"His business was Amos Hale's farm. A whole year he's been after it, and a whole year Amos told him where he could put his money." She shuts the register drawer. "Then Amos goes under a tractor. Half the people in this room will tell you that man put him there."

I look back at the table by the window. The two old men are looking at me.

The whole room just watched me eat supper with him. They watched me laugh.

And he never asked my name. He never asked what I was doing in Marlow. I thought he was being polite.

He wasn't being polite. He already knew.


**CHAPTER 3**

Sam Tanner is standing in front of the lawyer's door.

I see him from a block away, because there's nothing else on Main Street to look at. At ten to ten in the morning, Marlow is a town with the sound turned off. The tavern is dark. Lottie's is open, and every chair that was taken last night is empty. The grocery has its lights on and nobody in it. Last night thirty people watched me eat. This morning there's nobody to watch me walk, and I can't decide which one I like less.

And now there's a man in a tan jacket at the one door I need.

What is he doing here?

His grandmother lives twenty-five minutes out of town, and his car is in the shop. He didn't wander by.

I put on my work smile. It's the one for customers who walk in a minute before we close.

"Sam. Are you seeing Mr. Lang too?"

"I'm seeing you." He grins. "Grandma's at the grocery. I told you I'd walk you in."

"And I told you no."

"You told me you'd been walking into rooms by yourself for a long time. I figured you were due a day off."

It's a good line. I think he worked on it in the Buick.

Yesterday, of all the seats on that bus, he took the one beside mine. Today, of all the doors in this county, he's at this one. And I haven't forgotten what he told me. His grandmother's land is worth nothing to that company unless the farm next door is sold along with it. In about an hour, somebody is going to walk out of this building owning that farm.

He isn't here for me. He's here for the news.

"It's a will, Sam. I don't think I get to bring a guest."

"Then he'll say so, and I'll go." The grin goes away. "Nora, those three have known each other all their lives, and Lang has known them longer than that. You'll be the only person in that room nobody knows. I couldn't sit out at the farm and think about it. Let me try."

He presses the buzzer before I can answer.

There's a brass plate beside it: EVERETT LANG, ATTORNEY AT LAW. Nothing comes out of the little speaker. A minute later something hums behind the door, and the lawyer opens it himself.

I've been building Everett Lang in my head since Milwaukee, and I built him too big. He's past sixty and gray all the way through: the suit, the hair, the skin. He looks tired in a way that sleep doesn't fix.

"Miss Bell." He doesn't offer his hand. He's already looking past me. "And Mr. Tanner. Is your grandmother ill?"

"She's fine. I'm here with Nora. I'm a friend."

"Since when?"

"Yesterday," Sam says.

"No." There's no heat in it. "This is the reading of a will. It is for the family and for the persons named in it. You are neither. You may wait here, or you may go and find your grandmother."

Sam looks at me. He shrugs and lifts his eyes at the same time: *I tried.*

"That's all right," he says. "I just wanted to help."

"Thank you, Sam."

I almost mean it. Mostly I'm grateful to the lawyer, and that isn't how I planned to start with him.

The elevator is the size of a closet, and it has one button.

"One floor," Lang says, as the door slides shut. "My clients are mostly past seventy." He takes a tube of tablets out of his pocket, thumbs one loose and chews it. "Did you have a good flight?"

"I came on the bus."

"Of course you did." He mailed me the ticket himself. "A long ride. I hope they stopped, at least. Along the way."

"Twice."

"Good. That's good."

He doesn't want to know. He's a man shut in a box with a stranger, and he's doing what you do. I've made that kind of talk over ten thousand cups of coffee. I know what it's worth, and I don't hold it against him.

I didn't come here to be liked. Somewhere upstairs there's a piece of paper with a number on it. I want to hear the number, sign for it, and be on the next bus north.

The door opens on a hallway with brown carpet and one door at the far end, with pebbled glass in it.

Somebody is shouting behind the glass.

At first it's only noise, a woman's voice and a man's. Lang doesn't hurry and he doesn't slow down. He has walked toward this noise before.

Then we're close enough for the words.

"—and why are you on your feet? Sit down, Wade. What are you going to do, hit me? Or would you like to kill me too?"

"Say that again." A man, deep and loud.

"Forty years he promised you that farm, and he never once put it on paper. And who found him? You did. All by yourself, at four in the afternoon."

"I'm the only one in this family who wanted that farm kept! You hadn't been home in ten years, Colleen. Then a stranger offers Dad eighty million dollars, and suddenly you can find the road. Twice in one week."

Eighty million dollars.

I came here hoping for a few thousand. Somewhere in eighty million there must be a few thousand for me. Nobody would even miss it.

"Could you two keep it down?" A second man, lighter, almost cheerful. "I don't think they heard you in Tolliver."

The deep voice laughs. It's a big laugh, and there's nothing behind it.

Lang opens the door.

The one laughing is on his feet. That's Wade. He's fifty-five or so, in a clean work shirt buttoned to the neck, and he's the biggest thing in the room. His eyes look as if he hasn't slept in a month.

The woman is in a chair. That's Colleen. She's about fifty, and everything on her is the real thing: the bag, the watch, the wool. I know her. She's the customer who sends the soup back. Only one thing on her isn't perfect. Her right thumbnail is bitten down to the skin.

The third one is leaning by the window with his hands in his pockets. That's Teddy. He's ten years younger than Wade, with a soft, friendly face, and he's the only one of them who looks as if he slept last night.

Then all three of them are looking at me.

Nobody says hello. Nobody offers a hand. Teddy gives me a small nod, as if we're waiting at the same bus stop, and that's the warmest thing in the room. I got more of a welcome at Lottie's.

A second ago Colleen was screaming. Now her face is as smooth as a plate. Her eyes go down me to my shoes and back up, and they rest for a second on my roots.

I know when I'm being priced.

"Miss Bell. There, if you would." Lang points to a chair at the far end of the table, by itself. The Hales have the whole other side.

I sit. I keep my bag on my knees and my mouth shut.

The room is brown: the table, the shelves, the rows of cardboard boxes with names on them in black marker. The biggest box says HALE. In the corner there's a safe, the old green kind, with a dial.

Lang hasn't reached his own chair when Wade says, "Well. Now it all falls into place."

"What falls, Wade? Into what?"

"Look at her." He isn't looking at me. He's looking at the lawyer. "Blonde. Pretty. Built like that. Forty years Dad never had a spare dollar for any of us, and I always said it was going somewhere. So here she is."

That's three men in two days who have added me up, and every one of them got the same answer.

"I have not read one line of this will," Lang says, "and you already have her in your father's bed."

"I disagree with my brother about nearly everything, Everett," Colleen says. "But I do have eyes."

Teddy comes away from the window and puts a hand on his sister's shoulder. "Can we all calm down? Let's hear the will. Then you two can kill each other in the parking lot."

"Fine by me," Wade says. "I'll listen all day."

Two minutes ago these people were calling each other murderers. I've seen it at work. A family fights all the way through dinner. Then the check comes, and they agree as one that the waitress got it wrong.

It's a coalition: three people who can't agree on anything except me.

Wade isn't finished.

"But I'm telling you up front, Everett. I don't have my sister's money. What I do have, I'll spend on this woman. Who she is. Where she's from. What she was after. I'll dig until I hit the bottom of her, and then I'll see her out of this county."

Lang has stopped with one hand on the back of his chair.

Wade turns his head. It's the first time he has looked at me.

Let them call me a mistress. I've been called worse by people who left a tip.

It's the digging I can't afford.


**CHAPTER 4**

Lang doesn't answer Wade.

He lifts his eyebrows and shakes his head slowly. He looks like a tired teacher with a boy who won't stop talking. Then he sits down, takes some papers out of a blue folder and starts to read them to himself.

"Built like that," Wade says again. "You see it too, Everett. Don't tell me you don't."

Lang doesn't look up. He is pretending Wade isn't there.

"Forty years, and not a dollar for any of us. Not for Mom's teeth. Not for Teddy's school."

"Wade," Teddy says. "Enough."

Wade stops. Only then does Lang look up at the three of them.

"May I read the will now?"

Nobody answers. So he reads.

He reads in a flat voice, like a man reading a phone bill. The beginning is lawyer language, and I understand about half of it. Sam warned me about this on the bus.

Then he comes to the part that matters, and he reads it slowly.

"I give all of my property, including the farm, to the living child of my daughter, Carol Bell, on condition that she reside upon the said farm for a period exceeding one year."

I go over it again in my head, in plain words.

The old man had a daughter. Her name was Carol Bell. Everything he owned goes to Carol's child. But she only gets it if she lives on the farm for more than a year.

Lang puts his hand flat on the paper. He closes his mouth and looks at the three of them. He is waiting.

For a long time nobody talks.

They are shocked. I can see it. But they aren't as shocked as I thought they would be. They look like people who heard a story about their father years ago, and didn't want to believe it.

Teddy is the first to speak. He's looking at the floor and shaking his head.

"Well."

Then he looks up at Lang. "This is— I mean— Dad had another daughter? And she—" He points at me without looking at me. "She's that daughter's girl?"

"That is what the will says," Lang says. "Miss Bell was not your father's mistress. She is his granddaughter."

His granddaughter.

Now all three of them turn and look at me.

I don't move. I don't let my face show anything.

Ten minutes ago these people thought I was the old man's mistress. Now they have been told I'm his granddaughter.

Inside, my head is spinning. Nobody ever told me any of this. Not one word.

And it isn't a few thousand dollars. It's the farm. The whole farm. The farm that a company wants to buy for eighty million dollars.

"I am astounded," Colleen says.

Wade turns on her. "Astounded? When was the last time you visited Dad?"

"Twice that week. You know I did."

"And before that?"

Colleen doesn't answer. She puts her left hand over her right hand. She's hiding the bitten thumbnail.

"Ten years ago," Teddy says quietly. He sounds tired. I think he has heard this fight many times.

Colleen looks at him. "What does it matter?"

"What does it matter?" Wade says. "What does it—"

He stops. He looks at me, and then at Lang. He doesn't want to do this in front of a stranger.

"I think we need some private time here," he says.

"You want to talk in here?" Lang asks.

"If that's possible."

"Of course." Lang stands up and comes to my chair. "Come, Miss Bell."

I stand. At the door he turns back to them. "Five minutes? Ten?"

"Ten," Teddy says. "Please."

Lang takes me out to the hallway and closes the door.

"Wait here," he says. "But not by the door. Sit on the bench over there."

The bench is at the other end of the hallway, by the elevator. He doesn't want me to hear what they say. I sit.

He goes back into the room. A few seconds later he comes out again. He walks down the hallway and stands next to my bench.

"Well," he says. "They threw me out too." He shrugs.

It's the first friendly thing he has said to me. So I take my chance.

"Mr. Lang, I don't understand any of this."

"What don't you understand?" He sounds tired of me already.

"All of it. Why didn't your letter tell me anything?"

"I don't put a will in the mail, Miss Bell."

"Then how did you find me?"

"I hired an investigator. It took him three weeks."

An investigator. So a man has already been through my life, and I'm still sitting here. He can't have looked very hard.

"So tell me now. What does it mean for me?"

He takes the tablets out of his pocket and chews one.

"I am not your lawyer. The court made me the executor of this will. That means my job is to carry out what Mr. Hale wrote. That is why the estate paid for your bus and your room. But I can only read you the will. I can't digest it for you."

"I only want to know—"

"I can't advise you, and I can't advise them. They can't hire me, and you can't either."

"Then just tell me one thing." I can hardly say it out loud. "If I live on that farm for a year, do I get eighty million dollars?"

"Eighty million dollars? I don't remember any number like that in the will."

He's right. He never read that number. I heard it through the door, when Wade was shouting. A stranger offered the old man eighty million dollars for the farm.

So if the farm is mine, I can sell it to that stranger. Can't I?

I ask him. He shakes his head.

"You are putting me in an uncomfortable position. I can't advise any side here. Hire a lawyer, Miss Bell. You'll need one."

A lawyer. I have forty dollars in my bag, and I'm three weeks behind on my rent.

For a while we don't talk. He looks at the door at the end of the hallway. Then he looks at me.

"May I ask you something? He was your grandfather. In all those years, you never came to see him?"

I have to answer, and I have to answer fast.

"I did. Twice." I look down at my hands. "You have to understand, he lived so far away. The first time was my sixth birthday. There was a cake with a tractor on it. The second time I was fourteen." I stop for a second. "I didn't like how he looked at me. I didn't like how he grabbed my arm in the kitchen. After that I didn't want to go back. And I never knew his name. For me he was just Grandpa. That's what my mother called him."

It's a lie. All of it. I never set foot on that farm. I never saw that man in my life.

But what could I say? The people in that room have just been told I'm his granddaughter. A granddaughter who never once met her grandfather would look strange to them. So I gave him two visits. Nobody can check them. The only man who could say I'm lying is dead.

And on the bus I told Sam that I never heard the name Amos Hale before the letter came. That was true, and Sam has told his grandmother by now. So I can't say I knew the name. That's why he was only "Grandpa."

The part about the looks is true, by the way. Men have looked at me like that since I was fourteen. Just not this man.

"I'm sorry to hear it," Lang says.

I think he believes me.

"Can they stop it?" I ask. "Can they say he was crazy when he wrote it?"

"They can say what they like. I saw Mr. Hale at first light on the day he died. I took him his crop insurance forms to sign, on my way to court. He read every line and argued about the price. His mind was perfectly clear, and he was perfectly rude. I will swear to both."

"You saw him that day?"

"Early. Then I was in court all day. At five o'clock they called me out of the courtroom and told me he was dead." He looks away. "That isn't advice, Miss Bell. It's what I will tell a judge."

So the old man knew what he was doing when he wrote that will. The lawyer will say so to a judge.

The door at the end of the hallway opens. Teddy puts his head out.

"It's okay," he calls. "We're done."

We go back in. Lang sits in his chair. I sit in mine, alone at the far end.

Wade is sitting down now. It's the first time I've seen him in a chair. His big hands are folded on the table.

"Everett," he says. "This is all new to us. We need time to think about it. You understand what we're going through, right?"

Lang nods, very slowly. He doesn't say anything.

"But the three of us agree on one thing." Wade looks straight at me. "We want a DNA test. If she's really Dad's granddaughter, let her prove it."

Lang doesn't answer. Nobody says a word.

A DNA test.

For one minute, I had eighty million dollars.

Was I really the old man's granddaughter? I didn't care. The lawyer said I was, and for eighty million dollars I wasn't going to argue. I was going to live my year on that farm, take the money and go.

But a DNA test doesn't care what a lawyer says. One test, and it's all gone.

It's like giving a kid an ice cream and taking it back after one lick.

I should have known. I've known it all my life, and for one minute I forgot. There is no free money.


**CHAPTER 5**

Lang lets the silence sit. Then he takes off his glasses.

"A DNA test," he says. "From whom, Wade? From me?"

"Don't be funny, Everett. Not from you. You set it up. You get the court to order it."

"I can't." He says it flatly. "I am the executor. My job is to carry out this will. I can't be the one who attacks it." He puts his glasses back on. "But you can. Hire a lawyer. Ask the judge in Tolliver for a test. I won't stand in your way."

"Test her against what?" Colleen says. "Dad's in the ground."

"The hospital in Tolliver kept a piece of him when they took his gallbladder out," Wade says. "They keep everything."

"How long does it take?" Teddy asks.

Lang shrugs. "Courts are not fast. Weeks. A couple of months."

Weeks. A couple of months.

I keep my eyes on the table. But inside me something lets go a little. A minute ago I thought the test was tomorrow. It isn't. It's weeks away, and only if a judge says yes.

"You wrote this thing, Everett," Wade says.

"I typed it. For what it's worth, I advised him against it. He told me to mind my spelling."

"When?" Colleen asks. "When did he write it?"

"The fourteenth of May. At this table, with two witnesses from the bank next door."

Colleen doesn't say anything. She puts her left hand over her right hand again.

"What happened in May?" Wade asks her.

"Nothing happened in May."

She says it too fast.

Lang turns to me.

"Now. Miss Bell. I need your decision."

"My decision about what?"

He rolls his eyes. A man past sixty, in a gray suit, and he rolls his eyes at me like a teenager.

"Whether you accept." He turns a page. "There is one more line. 'The said period to begin on the day this will is read.'" He looks at his watch. "That was half an hour ago. So the year has started. Mr. Hale wrote 'reside'. He did not write 'visit'. If you accept, you sleep in that house tonight."

He takes one sheet of paper out of the blue folder, puts it in front of me, and lays his pen on it.

"If you would rather not, there is a form. One signature. The farm goes to Mr. Hale's children, and you are on the afternoon bus."

On the other side of the table, nobody moves. Colleen is looking at that sheet of paper the way I looked at the roast beef plate last night.

"Tonight?" I say. "I have to go back to Milwaukee first. All my things are there."

"Let me say this again, slower, because I don't think you heard it." He leans back in his chair. "The year started today. Not next week. Not when you have packed your things. If you sleep in Milwaukee tonight, you are not living on that farm, and it goes to these three."

"I just—"

"Of course, the place may be too big for you." His voice doesn't change. "Twelve hundred acres. Cattle. You may not know what to do with all of that. If so, the pen is in front of you."

Colleen almost smiles.

"And don't think you can come and go," Lang says. "In their place, I would pay a man to sit at the end of that lane and count the nights you sleep there."

Wade looks at Colleen. Colleen looks at Wade. It's the first time all morning they've looked at each other without hate.

I feel my face go hot.

"But I have a job," I say.

Lang's hand comes down on the table. Not hard. Hard enough.

"Miss Bell. Put what you earn carrying plates on one side. Put that farm on the other. Then tell me about your job." He lifts his hand. "Or go back to your tables. Nobody here will stop you. These people will be delighted, and I can close this file by Christmas."

Nobody says a word.

So I think it through. It doesn't take long.

In Milwaukee I have a room over a dry cleaner's, and I'm three weeks behind on the rent. Everything I own fits into two boxes, and my landlord will keep them for what I owe him. I have a job carrying plates, and Denise has my shifts. If I'm not back by Friday, my boss will give them to her for good.

Here I'd have a roof, for free. A bed, for free. And in a year, a farm that a company wants to buy for eighty million dollars.

There's only one problem: the test.

I know what I think about free money. There isn't any. So somebody has made a mistake. A man was paid to find a woman, and he found one. That doesn't make her the right one. And a DNA test is exactly the thing that finds a mistake like that.

But maybe they'll never ask a judge for it. Lawyers cost money, and Wade told the whole room that he doesn't have much. And if they do ask, it takes weeks. Until then I live in that house. I eat, and I sleep in a warm bed. That's already more than I have in Milwaukee.

And if a judge does order a test, I won't wait for the answer. I'll be on a bus north the same day. When a test says no, nobody says sorry and sends you home. They ask how the wrong woman got into the house. They start digging. And the digging is the one thing I can't afford.

"If I live out there," I say, "how do I buy food?"

"You won't go hungry. The estate pays for the house. And there is a monthly allowance. It's in the will." He sounds bored.

"Of course there is," Colleen says.

There is one more thing, and I don't like it at all.

Last night a whole diner told me that nobody believes Amos Hale died in an accident. If somebody killed him for this farm, that somebody could be sitting at this table. And I'm the woman who just took the farm away from them. Tonight I'll be sleeping in the dead man's house, half an hour from town.

I look at them. Wade with his big folded hands. Colleen with her bitten thumb. Teddy with his soft face, looking out the window.

Then I think about forty dollars.

I push the form back across the table.

"I'll move in today."

Lang looks at the form for a second. Then he puts it back in the blue folder. He sighs like a man who has just been handed a year of extra work.

Wade stands up. His chair scrapes on the floor.

"I can see that porch from my kitchen," he says to me. It's the second time he has spoken to me.

He stops at the door.

"So I'll know if your light is on."

Colleen picks up her bag and follows him out without a look. Teddy goes last. At the door he gives me the same small nod he gave me when I came in.

Then I'm alone with Lang.

He takes a thick envelope out of the folder and slides it down the table to me.

"A copy of the will. Read it."

"I read everything twice."

"Then you'll be the first." He's already reading something else.

"How do I get out there?"

He sighs, the way a man sighs when the phone rings at dinner.

"There's a county bus. The 897. It leaves from the gas station at 12:12 every day and goes out along the river road. Ask the driver for Hale Lane."

"And then?"

"And then you walk up the lane." He looks at me over his glasses. "Miss Bell, do I look like a help desk to you? There are people down there in the town. Ask them. Buy yourself something to eat, wait for your bus, get on it. That is not the problem."

"What's the problem?"

"The problem is that if you don't want to live out there, you should say so now and save everybody a great deal of trouble. Go back to Milwaukee, back to your work. That's a good choice too." He closes the folder. "Not everything in life is about money."

It's a strange thing to hear from a lawyer.

He picks up his phone. "I'll tell Mrs. Pruitt to make up a bed."

"Who is Mrs. Pruitt?"

"The housekeeper. Forty years in that house. The will keeps her there, so don't try to send her away." He's already dialing. "She won't thank me for this call."

I leave him to it.

---

It's ten past eleven when I come out onto Main Street. My bus leaves in an hour.

I'm halfway to the motel when the door of Lottie's opens and Sam Tanner comes out, pulling on his tan jacket.

He was sitting at the window. From that window you can see the lawyer's door. He has been watching it for more than an hour.

Yesterday it was the bus. This morning it was the lawyer's door. Now this. Wherever I go in this county, Sam Tanner is already there.

"Well?" he says. "How did it go?"

"Fine."

"Fine. That's all I get?" He grins. "I saw the Hales come out. Wade almost took the door off his truck. People who just got a farm don't look like that."

"I have to catch a bus, Sam."

"Which bus?"

"The 897."

The grin goes away. "The 897 goes out the river road. There's nothing on it but Grandma's place and Hale Lane." He looks at me. "You're going out to the farm."

I don't answer.

"That bus doesn't leave for an hour. And it drops you at the mailbox. From there it's two miles of gravel up to the house." He takes a set of keys out of his pocket. "I've got the Buick. Grandma's getting her hair done till one. I'll drive you."

I think about an hour on a bench at a gas station, and two miles of gravel with a suitcase.

He isn't coming into that house with me. I'll get rid of him in the yard.

"Okay," I say. "Thank you."

He waits outside the motel while I get my suitcase from room nine. When I put it on the back seat, he looks at it, and then at me. He doesn't say anything until we're out of town.

The town ends all at once. After that it's flat fields in every direction, cut down to stubble, brown and gray. It's the end of October, and the wind comes across those fields with nothing to stop it.

"Nobody takes a suitcase to look at a house," Sam says.

I look out the window.

"He left you the house." He turns his head, and he looks at me for so long that the Buick drifts toward the ditch. "Nora. Did Amos Hale leave you the whole farm?"

If I tell him, his grandmother will know by one o'clock, and the whole county by supper. But he's driving me to the door with my suitcase on his back seat. He has it already.

"I'm going to live in the house for a while," I say. "That's all I know."

He whistles, the same whistle as on the bus.

"So I was right about you."

"No. You weren't."

"Then why you?"

"I don't want to talk about it, Sam."

He's quiet for a mile. I know what he's thinking about.

Yesterday that farm was going to three Hales, and two of them wanted to sell it. His grandmother only had to wait. Today it's going to a stranger, and nobody knows what she'll do with it. So Sam Tanner needs to find out. Until he does, he's going to be very nice to me.

"Would you sell it?" he says. "If it was yours?"

"It isn't mine."

"In a year."

"Ask me in a year."

He laughs. I let him. Then I ask him the thing I've wanted to ask somebody since last night.

"Everybody in that diner knew who I was. How?"

"Teddy Hale. The three of them were told weeks ago that there'd be a stranger at the reading. A woman from Milwaukee. Teddy told the tavern the same night, and that's the same as telling the county."

"And the man from the data center?"

"Julian Cross?" He looks at me again. "You had supper with Julian Cross on your first night in town. Grandma knew before breakfast."

So that's how he knew me. Teddy told the tavern, and the tavern told him. And now the man has a name.

"Did Lang tell you about Agnes Pruitt?" Sam says.

"He said she won't thank him."

"She never thanked anybody. She didn't like Amos, and she ironed his shirts for forty years."

He turns off the road onto gravel. There's no sign, only a mailbox with HALE painted on it by hand. The lane runs between two fences for a long way. Far ahead, on a little rise, there's a white house with a porch.

Sam slows down and points to the left.

"That's the field. That's where Wade found him."

The field runs all the way down to the river. Along one edge there's a single dark strip of plowed earth. The rest of the field hasn't been touched.

"Nobody's been in it since," Sam says.

He stops in the yard and reaches back for my suitcase. "I'll carry it up."

"No." I take it from him. "Thank you for the ride, Sam."

He looks at the house, and then at me. "Tanner. We're in the book."

He turns the Buick around and drives back down the lane. I watch the dust follow him all the way to the road.

Then it's quiet. I didn't know a place could be this quiet. There's the wind, and somewhere a cow, and nothing else.

I carry my suitcase up the porch steps. There's a screen door, and behind it a wooden door, shut. I knock on the frame.

Nothing.

I knock again, harder. "Mrs. Pruitt?"

Inside, I hear steps. Slow ones. They stop on the other side of the door.

For a long time nothing happens. She's standing a few inches away from me, behind the wood, and she isn't opening.

Then a lock turns. The door opens three inches and stops.

There's a chain on it. The chain is new. The brass is still shiny.

In the gap there is half a face. It's lower than I expected, because she's a small woman. I can see white hair pulled back tight, one white eyebrow, and one eye.

The eye is pale, and it doesn't blink. It goes over me slowly. My shoes. My suitcase. My coat. It goes up to my hair and stops at the dark roots, the way Colleen's eyes did.

Then it comes down to my face, and it stays there for a long time.

I've been looked at all my life. Colleen looked at me like a price tag. This is different. This is how I look at a customer who walks in a minute before we close.

"Mr. Lang telephoned," she says. Her voice is thin and old. "He says I'm to give you a bed."

The eye goes over my face one more time.

"He didn't say tonight."

The door shuts. I hear the lock turn.

I stand on the porch with my suitcase.

The year started this morning. I have to sleep in this house tonight, or the farm goes to the Hales. And the only person inside it has just locked me out.


**CHAPTER 6**

"Mrs. Pruitt? I know you're still there."

Nothing.

But I heard her come to the door, and I haven't heard her leave it. She walks slowly. I would have heard.

So she's standing on the other side of that wood, a few inches from me. She's waiting for me to give up and go.

Go where? Sam is gone. The bus stops two miles from here. And I have until tonight to get into this house. If I don't, the farm goes to the Hales, and I go back to Milwaukee with forty dollars.

I take out my phone anyway. It's the cheap kind from a drugstore. You pay for the minutes before you use them, and I have a few dollars left on it. I still have Sam's number, on the back of a bus ticket. I could call him back.

But where the little bars should be, the screen says NO SERVICE. There's no signal on this farm.

I sit down on my suitcase and take the thick envelope out of my bag. Then I put on my reading glasses. They cost three dollars at a drugstore. Without them, a page of print is gray fog.

Lang told me to read it. All right. I'll read it.

It's eleven pages, and the wind keeps trying to take them out of my hands. Most of it is lawyer language. I read every line anyway.

On page six I find what I need. It's two paragraphs, one under the other.

I stand up and go to the door. I read the first one out loud, slowly, the way Lang reads.

"'Agnes Pruitt, my housekeeper, shall keep her room in the said house, and shall be paid her wages, for as long as she keeps the said house.'"

Nothing moves behind the door.

"I think you know that one," I say. "Here's the one under it. 'The said child shall have a room in the said house, and shall sleep in it from the first night.'"

I wait.

"That's me, Mrs. Pruitt. And the first night is tonight. You said Mr. Lang didn't say tonight. He didn't have to. Mr. Hale said it."

It's true. This morning I thought the hurry was the lawyer's. It isn't. The old man wrote it down himself: the first night.

Still nothing. So I say it in plain words.

"This paper gives you your room, and it gives me mine. You keep your room for as long as you keep his house. And he wanted me in his house, starting tonight. He wrote that down. So if you lock me out, you're breaking his will. Then Mr. Lang doesn't have to pay you anymore. And you don't have a room either."

Is that how the law works? I have no idea. I carry plates for a living. But she irons shirts, so neither of us is a lawyer.

"There's a telephone in there," I say. "Call Mr. Lang and ask him. I'll wait."

She doesn't call him. I didn't think she would. He's the one who told her to give me a bed.

I know what I've just done. I've told an old woman that she could lose her home. She has lived in this house for forty years, and I don't think she has anywhere else to go. I'm sorry to use it on her.

I use it anyway.

The chain rattles. The door opens.

She's even smaller than I thought. The top of her head comes up to my shoulder. She must be close to eighty, and there's nothing left on her but bones. She's wearing a gray dress, a white apron, and a gray cardigan buttoned up to the neck. Her hands hang in front of the apron. The knuckles are swollen, and the hands shake a little, all the time.

Only the eyes aren't old. They're pale, and they don't blink.

Looking at them, I think of something I should have thought of this morning.

I told the lawyer I came to this farm twice. Once when I was six, and once when I was fourteen. I told myself nobody could check it, because the only man who could call me a liar was dead.

I forgot about her. This woman has kept this house for forty years. If a little girl had come here for her sixth birthday, this woman would have baked the cake.

She knows no girl ever came. And all the lawyer has to do is ask her.

"Wipe your feet," she says.

I wipe my feet.

The front door doesn't open into a room. It opens into a narrow hallway, dark and brown, with a staircase going straight up in front of me. It's as cold in here as it was on the porch, and it smells of floor wax.

One coat hangs on the hooks by the door. It's a man's barn coat, big and brown, with the collar worn pale. I don't have to ask whose it is. Nobody has moved it in a month.

On my right there's a wide doorway, and through it I can see the living room. Nobody is living in it. Every chair is under a white sheet. So is the sofa. So is something tall in the corner, about the size of a person. I tell myself it's a clock.

"The front room isn't used," Mrs. Pruitt says.

"Since when?"

"Since Mrs. Hale."

She says it the way other people say "since the war."

On my left there's one door, and it's shut. It has a lock on it, a big black iron one.

"That is Mr. Hale's study," she says. "His office. He did his accounts in there. It is locked, and it stays locked."

"What's in it?"

"His desk and his papers. After he died, Mr. Lang came out here and made a list of every paper in that desk. Then he told me to keep the room locked, so that nothing goes missing. If anything goes missing, I'm the one who answers for it." She puts her hand on the pocket of her apron, and something in it clinks. Keys. "There is one key to that door. I carry it."

I know that sound. Keys in somebody else's pocket. I listened to it for a long time, in a place I don't talk about.

Under the stairs there's a little table with a black telephone on it.

"The telephone is for the house," she says. "I answer it."

My own phone is dead out here. So that black telephone is the only way to reach anybody outside this farm. And she has just told me it's hers.

"Yes, Mrs. Pruitt."

At the far end of the hallway a door stands open, and yellow light is coming out of it. That's the kitchen. It looks warm. She doesn't take me there.

She looks at my suitcase instead.

"I don't carry," she says. She lifts her hands an inch to show me why.

"I'll manage."

In Milwaukee there was always a bus, or a cab, or an elevator. Here there's a staircase, and my two arms.

The suitcase only holds two nights of clothes, and it still feels as if it's full of bricks. The stairs are narrow and steep. I have to carry it in front of me with both hands, and it bangs against my knees on every step.

Mrs. Pruitt goes up ahead of me, one step at a time, holding the rail with both of her shaking hands. Halfway up she stops to get her breath.

This woman couldn't carry my suitcase up these stairs if her life depended on it. I don't think she could carry it across the hall.

Then she does something strange. She lifts her foot high over the next step and puts it down on the step after that. She doesn't touch the one in between.

I don't think about it. I put my foot on that step.

It cracks under me, as loud as a gunshot.

I nearly drop the suitcase. Mrs. Pruitt doesn't even turn around.

"That step," she says. She points down at it with one bent finger. "The ninth one. When anybody stands on it, it makes that noise. Mr. Hale would never let anybody fix it. He liked to know when somebody was on his stairs at night. He could hear it from his bed."

"And you step over it."

"For forty years."

I count the rest of the way up. There are thirteen steps, and only the ninth one makes a noise.

So all those years, nobody could go up or down these stairs at night without the old man hearing it. Nobody except a person who knew which step to skip.

I'm going to remember that.

Upstairs there's another hallway, with five doors. All of them are shut.

"The bedrooms," she says. "There's no key to any of them. Mr. Hale took the bedroom keys away when the children were small. He didn't want any locked doors in his house."

"But his study downstairs is locked."

"The study was his. Everything else in this house, he said, was everybody's."

So in this whole house only one door locks: the study downstairs, the dead man's office. And the only key is in her apron pocket.

Up here, nothing locks. I can't open his door, and I can't lock mine. Anybody in this house can walk into my room at night.

The last door is at the far end. She opens it and stands back.

"You'll be in here."

It's a narrow room with one window, an iron bed, a small table, a chair and a rag rug. Under the window there's a radiator, the metal kind that gets hot. I put my hand on it. It's as cold as the wall.

"The furnace quit two weeks ago," she says.

I've always lived in apartments. I have to ask. "The furnace?"

She looks at me as if I'd asked her what a cow is. "The big heater down in the cellar. It heats water and sends it up to the radiators in every room. It's broken. Mr. Hale never paid to fix anything, and now he's dead, and nobody else has fixed it either. So there's no heat upstairs at all."

"And downstairs?"

"In the kitchen I have the cooking stove. It burns wood. I cook on it, and it keeps the kitchen warm. That's the only warm room in this house."

So she has a warm kitchen. I have a coat from a church thrift shop.

"Whose room was this?"

"Mrs. Hale's. He moved her in here when she took sick, so she wouldn't keep him awake." She looks at the bed. "She died in it. The eighth of February, forty-one years ago. I came to this house in the spring. Nobody has slept in that bed since."

I look at the bed too.

I go over it in plain words. The will says the heir is the child of a daughter named Carol Bell. Bell, not Hale. A woman can get a new name by marrying, I know that. But this morning Teddy asked the lawyer, "Dad had another daughter?" The three of them had never heard of Carol. So she didn't grow up in this house, and Mrs. Hale wasn't her mother. Her mother was some other woman.

There are five bedrooms up here. And for me, the housekeeper has picked the bed that Mrs. Hale died in.

I don't think she ran out of rooms. To her, I'm the other woman's family. The bed is her way of telling me so.

It's only a bed, I tell myself. The sheets are clean, and it's free. I've slept in worse.

I look at anything except the bed. That's how I see the hole.

It's in the floor, between the bed and the wall. It's square, about the size of the tray I carry four dinners on, and it has an iron grate over it.

I get down on my knees and look through the bars.

I'm looking down into another room. I can see the top of a small black iron stove, a piece of brown rug, and one corner of a desk. There's nothing on the corner of the desk. That's all the hole will show me.

"What's down there?"

"Mr. Hale's study. The locked room."

I look up at her. "There's a hole in my floor, and it goes into the locked room?"

"It's for the heat," she says, the way you explain something to a child. "There's a wood stove in the study. Mr. Hale lit it every evening when he sat at his desk. Hot air goes up. It comes up through that hole and warms this room. Every old farmhouse has a hole like that."

"So if somebody lights that stove, my room gets warm."

"Nobody has lit it since he died."

"And the other bedrooms?"

"They have no hole, and no stove under them." She looks at the grate. "But the hole doesn't only let the heat up. Sound comes up too. Mr. Hale could sit at his desk and hear every word that was said in this room. He could hear her turn over in her bed."

"Can it be closed?"

"It rusted open before I came."

I've rented rooms with mice, and rooms with no heat. This is my first one with a hole in the floor.

I stay on my knees a little longer.

If he could hear up, then I can hear down. That's something. But it works both ways. Anybody who stands in that room can hear me.

Do I talk in my sleep? I don't know. Nobody has slept near me in six years.

There are things I never say when I'm awake. I'd like to be sure I don't say them asleep.

I could pull the rug over the hole. I don't. If something happens in that room, I want to know about it.

She gives me the rest from the doorway. Supper is at six. Breakfast is at six. She doesn't cook twice. And the chain goes on the front door when it gets dark.

"If you're outside when it goes on, you're outside."

None of this is in the will. I've just read all eleven pages of it.

"Yes, Mrs. Pruitt," I say.

I've worked in restaurants for years. When a customer is rude to the kitchen, the cook spits in his soup. I've seen it done. So I'm not going to fight with the woman who cooks my food.

She hasn't called me anything, I notice. Not Nora, not Miss Bell. To her I'm not a person. I'm something the lawyer sent.

She shuts the door. I listen to her go down the stairs, one step at a time, with her keys clinking.

The ninth step doesn't make a sound.

I unpack. It takes four minutes. Then I push my suitcase under the bed, and it hits something.

I get down on the floor and look. There's another suitcase under there.

I pull it out. It's brown and stiff, with two metal locks, and it's older than I am. There's no dust on it. That's the strange part. Mrs. Pruitt has cleaned under this bed for forty years, and every time she has put this suitcase back where it was.

I shouldn't open it. I know that.

I open it.

It's packed. There are two dresses, folded flat. A nightgown. A hairbrush, with a few brown hairs still in it. And in one corner there's a pair of shoes, so small that both of them fit in my hand. They're a little boy's shoes, brown, with the toes worn white.

Mrs. Hale had three children. I met them this morning. She packed shoes for one.

A mother who takes one child with her and leaves the others behind. I know something about that. I'm not going to think about it in this room.

There's a pocket inside the lid. I put my hand in it, and I find a small piece of cardboard that has gone yellow.

It's a bus ticket. MARLOW TO INDIANAPOLIS. ONE WAY. Nobody ever tore it.

There's a date stamped on it. I put my glasses back on to read it. February 9. Then I read the year, and I do the sum. Forty-one years ago.

The ninth of February.

Mrs. Pruitt said the eighth.

So Mrs. Hale packed a bag, and she bought a ticket out of this house for the ninth of February.

On the eighth, she died in this bed.


**CHAPTER 7**

"Miss Colleen telephoned," Mrs. Pruitt says.

She says it without turning around. She's at the cooking stove with her back to me, and I've only just come through the kitchen door.

I stepped over the ninth step on my way down. She heard me anyway. So her ears aren't old either.

It's six o'clock. The kitchen is at the back of the house, and it's the only warm room I've found. There's a big black iron cooking stove that burns wood, with a pot on top of it, and a long table. At the head of the table stands one chair with arms. All the other chairs are plain. Beside the stove there's a door that must be her own room.

"Telephoned about what?"

"About you." She points her spoon at the wall by the back door. A calendar from the feed store in Marlow hangs there, with a pencil on a string. "I'm to mark every night you sleep in this house. And every night you don't."

So that's the man Lang talked about, the one who would sit at the end of the lane and count my nights. He turns out to be an old woman with a pencil, and Colleen gets her for free.

At least now I know the rule. The days are mine. I can walk around the farm. I can go to town. But every night I have to be upstairs in that bed before the chain goes on. If I miss one night, it goes on that calendar, and Colleen takes the calendar to a judge.

And what if this woman marks a night I didn't miss?

"What if you make a mistake?" I ask.

She turns around.

"I write down what is so. I always have."

I'm going to keep a count of my own anyway. I know what happens when there's only one set of books.

In the corner of the kitchen there's a narrow door, held shut with an iron hook. She takes a bowl from the shelf, lifts the hook and goes down into the dark.

It's the cellar. I can see the first few steps. They're as steep as a ladder, and there's no rail to hold on to. She doesn't turn on a light. All afternoon she has moved as if every step hurt her. Not on these stairs. She goes down them quickly, with one hand on the wall.

While she's gone, I go to the window over the sink.

The sun is low, but there's still light enough to see. The window looks down a slope at a big field, and I know that field. Sam showed it to me from the lane. There's the one dark strip of plowed earth along its edge, and the fence at the bottom. In half an hour it will be too dark to see any of it.

That's where the old man died, and this window looks straight at it. If somebody stood down there right now, I would see him. I wouldn't see his face. It's too far for that. But I would see that somebody was there.

And this is where she stands. At this sink, at this window, every day for forty years.

"Forty years on those stairs."

She's back, holding the bowl in both hands. It's full of pickled beets now. She isn't even out of breath.

"I know every one of them. You don't." She pushes the door shut and drops the hook back into its ring. "They would kill a person who didn't know them. You stay out of my cellar."

"Yes, Mrs. Pruitt." Then I say, "You can see the field from here."

She puts the bowl on the table.

"Were you in this kitchen that day?"

"I'm in this kitchen every day."

"Did you see what happened?"

She comes to the window. She reaches past me with one shaking hand and pulls the curtain shut.

"Mr. Wade found him," she says. "I told the sheriff I saw nothing."

She goes back to the stove.

I stand there, looking at a curtain.

She didn't say, "I saw nothing." She said, "I told the sheriff I saw nothing." Those are two different sentences. I know, because I've used the second kind myself. You use it when the first kind would be a lie.

So this woman saw something in that field. And she kept it from the sheriff.

What did she see? Who is she keeping quiet for? I don't know. I know only two things about her. She saw something on the day Amos Hale died. And she wants me out of this house.

And she has just cooked my supper.

"Sit," she says. She points to a plain chair at the side of the table.

She fills a plate from the pot and puts it in front of me. It's chicken and dumplings. Then she stands by the stove with her hands folded over her apron.

"Aren't you eating?"

"I've eaten."

I didn't see her eat. There's one pot and one plate of food, and the plate is mine.

I look at the dumplings.

What stops her? I'm half an hour from town. Nobody out here knows me, and nobody would come looking. If I'm gone, the farm goes to the Hales.

Then I think about it the other way. A month ago the man of this house died under his tractor, and the sheriff wrote down an accident. Now I'm the stranger who got his farm. If I fall over dead at his kitchen table, even that sheriff can't write "accident" a second time. Mrs. Pruitt is old. She isn't stupid.

Also, I haven't eaten since last night.

I eat. It's the best thing I've tasted in a year. If it's poisoned, I'd still like the recipe.

She watches me from the stove.

"Did you find everything you need in your room?"

She asks it politely. But she's looking at me hard.

She has cleaned under that bed for forty years. She knows what's there. And of all the beds in this house, she gave me that one.

She wanted me to find that suitcase. I'm almost sure of it.

"Everything," I say.

I don't tell her I opened it. Let her wonder.

I'm halfway through the plate when I look up the table.

A second place is laid at the head of it, in front of the chair with arms. There's a clean plate. A knife, a fork and a glass. A napkin, ironed and folded.

"Is somebody else coming?"

"No."

"Then who is that for?"

"Mr. Hale sat down to his supper at six o'clock for sixty years."

I look at the empty plate. Then I look at her.

"Sam Tanner told me you didn't like him."

"The Tanner boy talks." She doesn't say it isn't true. "I kept Mr. Hale's house for forty years. Nobody asked me to like him."

"Then why lay his place?"

"Because it is his place."

So it isn't love. I didn't think it was. Nobody irons a napkin every night for a dead man she couldn't stand.

Then who is the plate for?

There are two people in this kitchen. One of them laid it. So it's for the other one. It's for me.

She's telling me something. This is his table, and that is his chair, and I'm the stranger on the plain chair at the side. The bed upstairs said the same thing.

It's a show. An old woman is putting on a show to scare me off this farm, because if I go, the Hales get it back.

I tell myself that. It helps a little.

"Mr. Hale is dead, Mrs. Pruitt."

"Yes. He died in that field you were just looking at." She walks to the empty chair, slowly, and moves the fork a quarter of an inch. "And Mrs. Hale died in the bed you're going to sleep in tonight. This was their house. It still is."

She looks at me with those pale eyes.

"Do you believe the dead know who is sleeping in their beds?"
