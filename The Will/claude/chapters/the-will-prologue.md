# THE WILL: Prologue

Draft 4, 15 November 2026. About 590 words. American spelling (plowed, tire, gray) from here on; the narrator is from Indiana and so are the readers.

**When:** Late September, the day Amos died. It is dawn, but the text never says so. Wet ground, cold, no sun.

The voice is Teddy's, but the text never says so. Every sentence is true for him. It never says "I found him" and never says "I killed him", and it does not mention the car that passed him on the lane.

---

**PROLOGUE**

Nobody can ever know I was here.

That is the first clear thought I have had since I walked into this field, and it is a terrible thing to think, because my father is lying ten feet away from me under the back wheel of his tractor.

The engine is still running. It shakes the whole machine, and the machine shakes the fence it has rolled into, and the sound goes out over the river as if nothing has happened. As if he has only climbed down for a minute.

I can see one of his hands. Palm up in the mud, open. In eighty-one years he never once opened it for any of us. I can see his green cap in the furrow. He has worn it since before I could read. I am not going to look at the rest of him again. I looked once.

"Dad."

It comes out of me like a cough. Nothing moves except the machine.

"Dad. Get up."

He does not get up. I should be on my knees in the mud. I should be running for the house, screaming for help. Instead I am standing here with my hands jammed into my pockets so that I cannot see them shake, and what I feel, God help me, is relief.

He frightened me every day of my life. This frightens me more.

He would hate this. Not being dead. The diesel. He counted every drop that went into that tank, and now it is burning for nothing. I could reach up and turn the key. Two seconds. I cannot make myself touch it.

Everybody told him this machine would kill him. The brake has been gone for twenty years. When the safety switch broke, he twisted the wires together rather than pay forty dollars for a new one, and from that day you could start her from the ground with her in gear, and she would walk straight over whoever was standing beside her. He told everybody to mind their own business.

Forty dollars.

He had only just started. There is one black strip of plowed earth behind the machine, and the rest of the field has not been touched.

*You'll get this land over my dead body.* He said it to all of us, every time one of us asked, for years. Well. There it is.

And last night I told him what I would do on his grave, and half the county heard me. I can see every face in that room. They will remember every word.

The house is right there on the rise, and the kitchen window looks straight down into this field. From up there you can see the shape of things: the tractor, the fence, a person standing beside them. You cannot see a face.

Then the screen door bangs, and a voice comes across the field, thin and old.

"Mr. Hale? Mr. Hale, is that you?"

She cannot see the ground from there. She can see the tractor, and someone standing beside it, and to her whoever stands beside his tractor is him.

I don't answer. I don't move. She waits. In forty years he never answered her, and she is used to it. The door bangs again, and she has gone back in.

I don't touch him. I don't touch the tractor. The car is on the lane by the gate, on gravel. Gravel does not hold a tire track. But the ground here is soft, and I can see every step I took coming in, one line of them across the wet earth. One line is better than two. I know that is not true. I do it anyway.

I leave the engine running. I walk back to the car in my own footprints.

## How the death looks, locked down

**What everyone believes (the accident).** Amos plowed one strip. At the end of the row something fouled the plow. He got down, cleared it, and restarted the tractor from the ground, standing beside the big rear wheel, with the tractor still in gear. The bypassed safety switch allowed that. The tractor lurched forward, the rear wheel went over him, and the machine rolled a few feet on and stopped against the fence with the engine running. The cap was knocked into the furrow. Old farmers die exactly this way every year.

**What really happened (story file, B9).** Lang waved him down at the end of the first strip. Amos stopped, left the engine running, and climbed down for the papers. Lang hit him on the head with the wrench from the toolbox, laid him in front of the rear wheel, put the tractor in gear, and let it roll over him to the fence. The cap came off with the blow. The crushing hid the blow.

**Open question for the story file.** A tractor in gear against a fence for eleven hours would normally stall. Options: the rear wheels turn slowly in the wet river-bank mud and dig themselves in; or a worn clutch slips at idle; or the fence gives a little and the tractor sits against it with the clutch slipping. Decide before chapter 48, where the eleven hours are read off the meter.

## Facts this prologue fixes for the rest of the book

- The narrator says "Dad" aloud, so the reader knows the voice is one of the three children. All three remain possible.
- Teddy's car stood on the lane by the gate of the river field, on gravel.
- The text gives no time of day. It is dawn, but the reader should take it for the afternoon, shortly before Wade "found" him at four. The dawn comes out in chapter 48 (the eleven hours), chapter 50 (Teddy) and chapter 54 (Lang).
- Mrs. Pruitt came to the porch at dawn, called "Mr. Hale?" towards the river field, got no answer, and went back in. She took the figure by the tractor for Amos. She also saw Teddy's car on the lane (story file, B10). She never tells the sheriff any of this. Amos never answered her in forty years.
- The children were afraid of their father. He never gave any of them anything.
- The forty-dollar safety switch and the twenty-year-old brake are on page one. Wade repeats them in chapter 15, and they kill Lang in chapter 58.
- The tractor's lights are on, and one strip is plowed behind it. This is the scene that Teddy later copies for his "ghost" (chapters 9 and 21) and that the hour meter confirms (chapter 48).
- Amos's age, 81, is stated.

## Material cut from draft 1, and where it goes

- Shoes a size too big, bent nails in a coffee can, supper eaten in coats, a column in his book for what each child cost him: Teddy's first visit to the kitchen, which has no chapter yet.
- The wife's death, the cheapest coffin, back in the field the same afternoon: Ruth, chapter 11. The story file records it in B1a.
- The thirty-year court case against the power company, asking monthly rent for the line that crosses one corner of the farm, still open at his death: Julian at dinner, chapter 16, or Wade at the barn, chapter 8.
- The lane that goes nowhere but the farm, and the half hour to town: used in chapters 2 and 5.
- Walking behind him in the cold as a child to get his attention: Teddy's first visit to the kitchen, or Wade, chapter 30.

## What changed in draft 4

15 November 2026, after the Kimi K3 reading of chapters 1 to 3.

- **The time of day is gone.** "This morning", the gray sky, "in a few minutes it will be light enough to see a face" and "in this light" are cut. In their place: the kitchen window looks straight down into the field, and from up there you can see a person but not a face. Nothing false was added. Every line is still true at dawn.
- **Why.** The whole book says Amos died in the afternoon. In chapter 4 Lang says he sat with Amos "at first light". A reader who knew the prologue was at dawn, and doubted that the voice was the killer, would ask who was in the field before, and Lang would have just answered. Now Lang's sentence hides in plain sight, and the eleven hours in chapter 48 surprise the reader as much as June.
- **The sunrise is not lost.** It moves to chapter 50, where Teddy tells it, and to chapter 54, where Lang remembers it.
- **Rules for later chapters.** Until chapter 48 June never says "morning" or "dawn" about the death. Nobody tells her before chapter 50 that Mrs. Pruitt called to "Mr. Hale" at first light. Mrs. Pruitt's "the morning Mr. Hale died" in chapter 31 stays: it is the first crack. Pictures and videos of the prologue are gray and wet, with no sun and no lit window.
