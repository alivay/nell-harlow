# How Freida McFadden builds a chapter

Written on 12 November 2026, from a close reading of chapter 1 of *The Housemaid*. Updated on 13 November 2026, after chapter 1 of *The Will* was written. These are the rules we follow when we write a chapter. The examples are described, not copied.

## What the chapter is

Millie is interviewed for a housekeeper's job by a rich woman, Nina. She is shown the house and her attic room, meets the daughter, and leaves sure that she will not be hired. The gardener watches her go.

It is about 2,400 words, which is twice our target. A first chapter is allowed to be long.

## The twelve rules

**1. Start inside a scene, with another person.**
The first line is the interviewer's question. There is no journey to the house, no weather, no waking up. The narrator is already in the room and already under pressure.

**2. Give the narrator a want and a reason she will fail, on the first page.**
She wants the job badly, and she is sure she has no chance. Every line after that is read through this want.

**3. She says one thing and thinks another.**
This is the engine of the chapter. Aloud she is agreeable: yes, of course, it's perfect. In her head she is sarcastic, doubtful or afraid. The reader is the only one who hears both, and that makes the reader her accomplice.

**4. One or two lines of speech, then her mind.**
The talk never runs long. After a line or two the narrator steps in and asks: what does this mean, what does this woman want, what does it cost me? The thought often contradicts what she has just said.

**5. Description always carries a feeling.**
Nothing is described neutrally. The blouse is described as something she wants to touch. The house is described by what it would cost. The attic room is described by comparing it with the back seat of her car. We learn what a thing looks like and what the narrator feels about it in the same sentence.

**6. People are described by one sharp judgment, not by a list.**
The employer gets a slightly cruel remark about what her haircut is hiding. The child gets one image: she looks as if she belongs in a horror film. The gardener gets muscles, tattoos and silence. Each person has one tag that the reader remembers.

**7. The narrator describes herself through a choice she made.**
She does not look in a mirror. She tells us that she dressed plainly on purpose, with fake glasses, so that the employer would not feel threatened. We learn how she looks, and we learn that she is calculating.

**8. Secrets are dropped as small asides and never explained.**
Her CV is doctored. She lives in her car. She has not been near children for ten years. A background check would finish her. Each one is a single sentence, said in passing. The reader adds them up and asks the question the book wants asked: what did she do?

**9. Unease, then an excuse, then she goes ahead anyway.**
The attic room frightens her and she cannot say why. She notices the thick door and the tiny window. Then she tells herself she would be lucky to have it. The same three steps happen with the child and again with the gardener. The reader sees the danger more clearly than she does.

**10. Jokes sit right beside the fear.**
She estimates the chance that the child will murder her, as a percentage. She imagines dollar signs in her eyes. The humour is exaggerated and aimed at herself. It makes her likeable, and it lets the dread come in without melodrama.

**11. The setting is a list of things that will matter later.**
The house tour is slow, but it is doing a job. It shows the whole map of the book and plants the attic room. The rich rooms exist to make the attic look worse.

**12. The ending is a small, silent, unexplained thing.**
First there is a false ending: she walks away sure it is over. Then the gardener looks at her and shakes his head, as if to warn her. Nobody speaks. It is a gesture, not an event, and it points the opposite way from what she wants.

## The language

- First person, present tense.
- Short paragraphs, often one or two sentences.
- Plain words. A reader never has to read a sentence twice.
- She asks herself questions in the middle of the narration.
- Almost no metaphors. When there is one, it is a joke.
- No block of back story anywhere.

## What she does not do

- She does not explain the narrator's past.
- She does not describe a person from head to foot.
- She does not let a scene pass without a second person in it.
- She does not end a chapter on a calm note.

## The plain-language rule (Aviel, 15 November 2026)

This rule comes before all the others. It applies from chapter 4 on.

An ordinary reader must understand every line the first time. Freida McFadden writes for that reader. If the reader has to work something out, many will miss it, lose the story and put the book down.

- **Say it, don't hint it.** If a look, a gesture or a silence matters, the narrator says what it means. "She puts her left hand over her right hand. She's hiding the bitten thumbnail."
- **No line that needs a second step.** "I liked it better when I was his mistress" needs the reader to work out why. "I'm scared" does not.
- **After legal or technical words, June says it again in plain words.**
- **Everyone who speaks is named.** A weaker test reader gave Colleen's lines to the lawyer.
- **A character says the important fact aloud.** "She is his granddaughter."
- **Simple pictures only.** "Like a tired teacher" is fine. A picture the reader must decode is not.
- **The secrets stay secret.** Plain language is about what happens on the page. June still never tells the reader a false thing, and she still does not say what she is hiding.

This replaces the old advice "do not explain after showing". For this book: show it, then say it.

## Aviel's rules of 16 November 2026

These apply to every chapter from chapter 6 on.

- **One thing learned, then a cliff.** Every chapter of about 1,500 words gives the reader one new thing that moves the story forward, and ends on a cliff. Everything else is filler: what June sees and what she thinks. The plan of a chapter names its "one thing" in bold.
- **No boring minute.** The filler must also hold something wrong or frightening that matters to the story. A quiet passage, such as June sitting and listening to the house, is cut.
- **The setting holds strange things that pay off later:** the step that cracks, the grate in the floor, the suitcase under the bed. Each one is listed in `planted-clues.md`.
- **Write for readers abroad too.** Explain what a city reader or a reader outside America would not know: what a furnace is, what a wood stove is, how an old farmhouse is laid out. When a character points at a thing or names it, say at once which thing it is and what it means.
- **Every chapter says its date** at the top of its file, and when time has passed the text says so ("Three weeks later"). The seasons must show, also for the pictures.
- **A plan is written in full sentences.** When a chapter is planned, each point says who does what and why. No shorthand.

## What the test readers added

- Do not repeat in the same words what the dialogue has just said. (The plain-language rule above replaces the older advice "do not explain after showing".)
- Keep the clever one-line sayings rare. Three in a chapter is plenty.
- Break up long speeches. Let the narrator interrupt, deflect or change the subject.
- Never let a character say that another character is honest. The reader takes it as proof that he is not.

## How chapter 1 of The Will uses the rules

The chapter is in `chapter-01.md`. Use this list as a check for every later chapter.

| Rule | In our chapter 1 |
|---|---|
| 1. Start in a scene | The first line is Sam's joke on the bus: "So what did you do?" |
| 2. Want and doubt | She wants a few thousand dollars. She is sure that free money does not exist. |
| 3 and 4. Says one thing, thinks another | "I wait tables", and what she leaves out. She keeps her face still when Sam names the dead man. |
| 5. Description with a feeling | The flat land: "you could watch a person coming for an hour". |
| 6. One tag for each person | Sam: the pale stripe where his watch was. Ruth: seen from the nose up. The clerk: a crossword in pen. |
| 7. Herself, through a choice | The coat she bought for the lawyer. The roots she could not afford to fix. |
| 8. Asides, never explained | The roommate who died. The cologne. "The expensive way." "Other places too." |
| 9. Unease, excuse, go ahead | The letter may be a scam, and she goes. Ruth stares, and she tells herself that waitresses get that. |
| 10. Jokes beside the fear | "Everybody's car is in the shop." "She tells the cat." |
| 11. The setting is a map | Sam's directions: Marlow, the county road, his grandmother's place, the Hale farm, the river. |
| 12. A small, silent ending | The clerk goes back to his crossword, and the pen is not moving. |
