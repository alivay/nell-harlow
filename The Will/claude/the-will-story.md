## PART A — THE PEOPLE

**June Mercer** is the narrator, 33 years old. She was a good bookkeeper until her boss stole from the company and arranged for her to take the blame. She spent three years in prison. For the last six years she has lived under the name of her dead roommate, Nora Bell, and worked as a waitress in Milwaukee. She bleached her dark hair blonde when she took the name. She is careful and suspicious, and she is very good with numbers and documents. Everyone in the book calls her "Nora" or "Miss Bell". She owns a cheap prepaid phone, and it has no signal at the farmhouse.

**Nora Bell** is the real Nora. She died six years ago at the age of 25. She was June's roommate for one year. She was also June's younger sister, but June never knew it.

**Amos Hale** is the farmer. He was 81 when he died. He was hard, proud, controlling and mean with money. He owned about 1,200 acres and refused $80 million for them.

**Mrs. Hale** was Amos's wife and the mother of Wade, Colleen and Teddy. She died in the farmhouse forty-one years ago, one day before she meant to leave him. Her first name is not fixed yet. See B1a.

**Everett Lang** has been the Hale family's lawyer for thirty years. He is in his sixties. In the book he is a grey, dry, correct man: polite to everyone and warm to no one. He chews stomach tablets. In chapter 5 he says he could close this file by Christmas. He spends the last weekend of every month at a fishing cabin, and he means to retire and fish when the estate is closed. He no longer comes to the farm in chapter 8, so somebody else has to tell June about the cabin before chapter 32. This is not placed yet. He is not June's friend, and he says openly that his sympathy is with the children. He appears in only a few scenes. He is the villain. He killed Amos, and he plans to take the farm through June.

**Wade Hale** is the eldest son, 55. He has worked the farm since he was a boy for almost no pay, because his father promised it would be his one day. He is big, silent and bad-tempered. He lives in a small house at the far end of the farm. The land is flat, so from his kitchen he can see the porch of the farmhouse and its lighted windows (chapter 5). He is June's open enemy at first and her ally at the end.

**Colleen** is the daughter, 50. She lives in the city, three hours away. She looks rich and is deep in debt. She fights June with lawyers.

**Teddy Hale** is the youngest son, 44. He lives in a rented flat in the town. He is charming, kind and weak. He once owned a small garage that fitted car alarms and remote starters. It failed. He drinks, and he owes money to dangerous people. He is the only Hale who is kind to June. He has two secrets. He is the one who starts the tractor at night. And he is the person who found his father dead at dawn and walked away.

**Mrs. Pruitt** is the housekeeper, about 80. She is small and frail, with swollen hands that shake, and her mind and her ears are sharp. She has lived in the farmhouse and run it for forty years. She does not own it. She read Amos's letters aloud to him and wrote his diary for him when his eyes became bad. She knows that June is not the girl in the photograph.

**Julian Cross** is in his late thirties. He buys land for the data centre company. He is good-looking, funny and patient. For a short time he becomes June's lover. Many things make him look guilty. He is not guilty of any crime. He is a businessman who protects himself in every direction.

**Ruth Tanner** is the only neighbour, 89. She owns 160 acres on the river, between the county road and the Hale farm, and drives an old Buick too fast. She loves to talk, and she remembers the old affair. The data centre company wants her land and the Hale farm together, or neither, so while Amos lived she could not sell. She and Amos could not stand each other for sixty years. Since his death she sleeps with the porch light on.

**Sam Tanner** is Ruth's grandson, about 35. He lives in Indianapolis and has lost his money: his car is gone and so is his watch. He visits his grandmother every three months to stay in her will. He sits beside June on the bus in chapter 1. He waits at Lang's street door in chapter 3, and in chapter 5 he drives her to the farm. He is charming, he asks too many questions, and he needs his grandmother's land sold. The Tanners gained from Amos's death and had no part in it.

**The sheriff** is an honest, slow, careful man. He is not part of any plot.

## PART B — BEFORE THE BOOK BEGINS

### B1. Amos and the hired girl

About fifty years ago Amos Hale was a young married farmer with a small son, Wade. One summer a nineteen-year-old girl called Dot Bell worked in the farmhouse kitchen. Amos had an affair with her and she became pregnant. Amos was afraid of the scandal and of his wife. He gave Dot some money, put her on a bus to a city far away, and told her never to come back.

Dot had a baby girl and named her Carol. She raised Carol alone and poor. For some years Amos secretly sent small sums of money. He was a man who wrote everything down, so he recorded these payments in his farm diary under the initials "D.B." Then he stopped paying.

Ruth Tanner, the neighbour, was a young woman at the time. She saw the hired girl leave suddenly and guessed why. She has never forgotten it.

### B1a. Mrs. Hale (added on 16 November)

Nine years after Dot Bell left, in the winter forty-one years ago, Amos's wife decided to leave him. Wade was 14, Colleen was 9 and Teddy was 3. She packed a suitcase with her own clothes and one pair of Teddy's shoes, and she bought a one-way bus ticket from Marlow to Indianapolis for 9 February. She meant to take the youngest child with her. Nobody knows what she meant to do about the other two.

That winter she had an infected tooth, and Amos would not pay for a dentist. This is the "Mom's teeth" that Wade shouts about in chapter 4. When the pain kept her awake, Amos moved her out of their bedroom into the narrow room over his study, "so she wouldn't keep him awake". Through the iron grate in the floor of that room he could hear every word she said.

The man who sold her the ticket told Amos. After that Amos would not send for a doctor either. He said that a woman who was well enough to run away was well enough to get up. The infection went into her blood, and she died in that bed on 8 February, one day before her bus. He bought the cheapest coffin and was back in his field the same afternoon.

Amos shut her room and let nothing in it be touched. He had sheets put over the furniture of the living room, and nobody has used that room since. Mrs. Pruitt came to keep the house in the spring. In her first week she found the packed suitcase under the bed and understood what it meant. She has cleaned around it for forty years. It is one reason she never liked Amos. She stayed for the children, and above all for Teddy, who was three.

**Who knows.** Wade remembers all of it. Colleen remembers that her mother was ill. Teddy remembers nothing and has been told little. Ruth Tanner knows the whole story and tells it to June in chapter 11: "He wouldn't pay forty dollars for her tooth, and he wouldn't pay forty dollars for that switch."

**What it does in the book.** It gives the three children an old reason to hate their father, so each of them is a better suspect. It is the root of the suitcase that Mrs. Pruitt packs for June and stands by the front door (chapters 10 and 13). And June, who was given away while her sister was kept, finds a suitcase in which a mother packed for one child out of three (chapter 6).

**Still open.** Her first name. And why she decided to leave that winter: one possibility is that she found the payments to "D.B." in Amos's book.

### B2. Carol's two daughters

When Carol was seventeen she became pregnant. Her mother Dot forced her to give the baby up for adoption. The baby was a girl. The family that adopted her named her June Mercer.

Two years later Carol had a second girl, by a different man. This time she kept the baby and named her Nora Bell.

So June and Nora are sisters with the same mother, and both are granddaughters of Amos Hale. They grew up in different towns and never met as children.

June always knew she was adopted. When she was fifteen her adoptive mother told her, during a quarrel, something the adoption agency had mentioned: her birth mother had later had another baby and kept that one. June has carried this all her life as a wound. She was given away, and the next child was kept. She never looked for her birth family and never wanted to.

### B3. How June went to prison

At the age of 21 June became the bookkeeper of a family company. The office was run by the owner's son-in-law, a charming man who seemed to like and trust her.

For two years he stole from the company. He invented suppliers that did not exist. Every month he gave June invoices from these false suppliers and said: "Just enter these and pay them." The payments went out under June's computer login, with June's approval.

He prepared the evidence in advance. Among the piles of company forms that he gave her to sign, he slipped in the papers for a new bank account. She signed them without reading them. So the account really was opened in her name, with her own signature. The stolen money passed through that account before it reached him. He also gave June a "bonus" for her good work and let her drive a company car. She was grateful.

When the company's auditors discovered the theft, everything pointed at June. Her login had approved the false invoices. A bank account in her name had received the money. She had a bonus and a new car. In court the son-in-law said sadly that he had trusted her completely. Her lawyer was cheap and overworked. The jury found it easy to believe that the bookkeeper did it.

June was 23 when she was convicted. She spent three years in prison and came out at 26. The son-in-law kept his job and the money.

She learned one thing there that shapes everything she does later: the person who controls the paperwork controls the truth, and a pleasant man with documents can destroy you.

### B4. Nora finds her sister

While June was in prison, Carol died. Nora cleaned out her mother's flat and found two things.

The first was the adoption papers. They told Nora that her mother had given away a first baby, and that she had an older sister called June Mercer.

The second was a bundle of old letters belonging to her grandmother Dot. They told the story of the affair and named the man: Amos Hale, a farmer.

Nora was already ill with a disease that would kill her in about a year. She was proud. She did not want to beg from a rich grandfather who had thrown her grandmother out. But she felt he should know that his daughter was dead. So she wrote him one short letter: "Your daughter Carol is dead. I want nothing from you. I only thought you should know." She put a photograph of herself in the envelope, so that he would see his granddaughter's face.

On the farm Mrs. Pruitt opened the letter and read it aloud to Amos, because his eyes were already bad. She held the photograph and looked at it for a long time: a dark-haired girl with a mole on her cheek and a crooked front tooth. Amos said nothing and never answered. But he kept the letter and the photograph in his desk.

Then Nora did the thing she most wanted to do before she died. She looked for her sister. She found June just as June came out of prison with no money, no job and nowhere to live. Nora did not say who she was. She offered June a cheap room in her small flat, as if by chance.

Nora meant to tell June the truth. But on their first evening June talked about her birth mother, the woman who gave her away and kept the next child. June said, with real anger: "I hope the one she kept knows what she cost me." Nora lost her courage. She was afraid that if she said "I am that girl", June would walk out, and she would die alone. So she stayed silent. The two women lived together for about a year and became very close. They looked a little alike, as sisters do. At the clinic where Nora was treated, the staff sometimes confused them.

### B5. The trade of names

Nobody would hire June. Any employer who checked her name found a convicted thief.

When Nora knew she had only a few months left, she made June an offer: "My name is clean, and I have six months. Your name is ruined, and you have fifty years. Let's trade."

June refused at first and then agreed. When Nora went into the hospice to die, she went in under June's name, with June's card. When she died, the death certificate said "June Mercer".

So on paper June Mercer, the convicted thief, is dead. And on paper Nora Bell, a woman with a clean record, never died. That is why June has been able to work, rent rooms and pay tax as Nora Bell for six years without any alarm. Nobody is looking for a dead woman, and nobody has reported Nora missing.

Nora's last words to June were: "You were always my sister." June heard only love in them. She did not understand that they were simply true.

Nora also left June a small brass key. It opens a safe-deposit box in a bank, which is a locked metal drawer that a customer rents. Nora had rented it years before, to keep her mother's papers safe: the adoption file and her grandmother's letters. In her last weeks she added a letter of her own, telling June the truth.

Why did she not simply tell her, or hand her the letter? Nora was brave about dying and a coward about this one thing. She could not bear to watch June read it, and she was afraid of spending her last weeks alone. So she left the choice to June. She gave her the key and said: "There are things in there about where I come from. Open it when you want to know. Not before."

June has never opened it, for two reasons. The first is fear. To open the box she would have to sign her name in front of a bank clerk, and the bank holds a card with the real Nora's signature. The second reason goes deeper. June has never wanted to know where anybody comes from, least of all herself. So it is June's own refusal to look back that keeps the truth from her for six years. She has carried the key all that time.

Nora died at 25. June was 27. That was six years ago.

### B6. The offer and the angry will

Now we come to the last year of Amos's life.

The Hale farm is about 1,200 acres of flat land. A river runs along one side and a main electricity line crosses one corner. A data centre is an enormous building full of computers. It needs a great deal of electricity and a great deal of water for cooling, so this land is exactly what such a company looks for. The company wants the whole bend of the river: the Hale farm and Ruth Tanner's 160 acres beside it, together or not at all. The company sent Julian Cross, and he offered Amos $80 million.

Amos refused, and so Ruth Tanner could not sell either. His favourite sentence, to Julian and to his own children, was: "You'll get this land over my dead body."

His children pressed him. Colleen and Teddy needed the money badly. Wade did not want the farm sold, but he wanted his father to put the old promise in writing at last. Amos gave none of them an answer.

Then Amos discovered that Colleen had quietly asked a lawyer in the city whether her father could be declared mentally unfit, so that the family could take control and sell. Amos was furious.

In that fury he remembered the letter in his desk. He called his lawyer, Everett Lang, and made a new will. This is what it says:

1. The farm goes to "the living child of my daughter Carol Bell".
2. There is a condition. She must live on the farm for a period exceeding one year, counted from the day the will is read. (Chapter 5 quotes the line: "The said period to begin on the day this will is read.") She must sleep in the house "from the first night" (chapter 6 quotes this from page six of the will).
3. During that year the farm does not belong to her yet. It belongs to "the estate", which means the dead man's property while it is being sorted out. The estate is managed by the executor, the person named in the will to carry it out. The executor is Lang. She receives a room in the house and a monthly allowance.
4. Mrs. Pruitt keeps her room and her wages "for as long as she keeps the said house" (chapter 6). Wade is kept on as farm manager and paid a proper salary.
5. If the granddaughter refuses, leaves before the year is complete, dies, or turns out not to be Carol's child, the farm goes to Amos's three children in equal shares.

The will is eleven pages long. Amos signed it on 14 May, at Lang's table, with two witnesses from the bank next door (chapter 5).

Two details matter.

**First, the wording.** Amos did not write the name "Nora Bell". He wrote "the living child of my daughter Carol Bell". Lang suggested using the name, and Amos answered: "Women change their names. Blood doesn't. Write it by blood." He thought about inheritance the way he thought about cattle.

This wording does two jobs in the story. Because the heir is described by blood, a blood test is the natural way to prove who she is, which is why the children can demand one. And at the very end of the book, this wording is what lets June inherit even though she is not Nora. If the will had said "to Nora Bell", the heir would be a dead woman. The gift would fail, and June would get nothing, even as a real granddaughter. But the will describes the heir by blood, and June is Carol's living child. The description fits her exactly.

**Second, why one year?** Partly it is a test: Amos wanted someone who would stay and work, not someone who would take the money and leave. Partly it is revenge: his children must watch a stranger live in their father's house for a year. He let the farm fall back to the children if she failed, because he was sure a real Hale would not fail. It was his last act of control.

Amos showed Lang the old letter and the photograph and said: "Find her." Lang made a copy of both for his investigator. Amos put the originals back in his desk, because he kept everything.

### B7. How a will is checked, and why Lang cannot simply cheat

A lawyer cannot do whatever he likes with a will.

Amos signed the will in front of two witnesses and a notary, an official who confirms signatures. After his death the will was handed to the probate court, the court that deals with dead people's property. The judge checked the signatures, accepted the will and officially appointed Lang as executor. From that day Lang must report to the judge: a list of everything Amos owned, and accounts of every dollar that comes in and goes out. The children have their own lawyer, who reads everything Lang files. If Lang changed the will or stole from the estate, he would be caught and would lose his licence.

So Lang does not touch the will, and he does not steal from the estate. On paper he does everything correctly for the whole year. His crime is planned for the moment after the estate is finished, when the farm belongs to the heir and she is free to sell it to anyone. A private sale by the owner is not checked by any court.

**Where it happens.** A farming county in southwest Indiana. Chapter 1 fixes it. Indiana has flat farmland, rivers and main power lines, and farms there really are being sold to data centre companies. Its county coroners are elected and do not have to be doctors, which the story needs. The town is Marlow and the county town is Tolliver, both invented. See `places.md`.

**A note on tax, so that nobody trips over it.** In America a very large inheritance is taxed, but only above about $15 million. For the court, the farm is valued as farmland, at about $14 million. The $80 million was one company's offer for one special use. Amos refused it, and it ended when he died. So no inheritance tax is due, and the book never has to mention tax at all. The $80 million exists only for the person who sells the land to the data centre.

**What happens when the year ends.** The farm does not become June's by itself at midnight. When her year is complete, Lang reports to the judge that the condition has been met. About ten days later the judge signs an order that gives her the farm, and Lang registers it at the county office. From that afternoon she is the owner and can sell. Lang has known this judge for thirty years and has every paper ready, so there is no delay.

### B8. What Lang discovered, and his plan

Lang hired a private investigator. It took him three weeks (Lang says so in chapter 4). The investigator traced Nora Bell through public records and found a woman of that name working as a waitress in Milwaukee. He sent Lang a photograph of her.

Lang put the two photographs side by side: the real Nora from the old letter, and the waitress. They were not the same woman. They were similar, but not the same.

Lang is a careful man, so he dug quietly by himself. He found that the waitress had once shared a flat with a woman called June Mercer, a convicted thief, who had officially died in a hospice. He found June Mercer's prison photograph. It matched the waitress.

He understood what had happened. The real granddaughter was dead. A convicted thief had been living under her name ever since.

He was right about the facts and wrong about the person. He assumed June was a real criminal: greedy, dishonest, the kind of woman who would gladly join a fraud for a share. He did not know she had been innocent of the theft.

This is how he reasoned.

If he tells Amos the truth, Amos changes his will again. The farm goes to the children or to someone else. Lang earns his usual fee and nothing more.

If he says nothing and the false heir inherits, he owns her. She can never refuse him, because one phone call to the sheriff sends her back to prison.

So his plan was this:

1. Tell Amos only: "We found her."
2. After Amos's death, bring her to the farm and make sure she is accepted as the heir.
3. Keep her on the farm until her year is complete.
4. When her year is complete and the judge has given her the farm, make her sign it over, on that same day, to a company he secretly owns, called Hale River Holdings, for $4 million.
5. His company then sells the farm to the data centre for $80 million. He keeps the difference, $76 million.
6. After she signs, she "leaves town". In plain words, he will kill her and make it look as if she took her money and went away. She has no family. Nobody will look for her.

### B9. The murder of Amos

Then Amos said something that would destroy the plan: "I want to see her with my own eyes. I'll drive up there next week. And I'll have a blood test done, to be sure she's Carol's girl."

If Amos met her, he might see that she was not the girl in the photograph. If he did a blood test, Lang believed it would show that she was no relation at all. Either way the plan would be dead.

So Lang decided that Amos must die before the trip, and that it must look like an accident.

The tractor matters, so here it is. It was fifty years old. Amos never paid to repair anything. The brake had not worked for twenty years. And when a small safety switch broke, he joined the wires around it to save forty dollars. That switch is the part that stops a tractor from starting while it is in gear. Without it, a tractor that is started in gear moves at once and runs over whoever is standing in front of its wheel. Everybody had told Amos that the machine would kill him one day. That is why nobody would doubt an accident.

Amos started work at first light every day of his life. On a morning in late September he was already in the river field, the field beside the river, ploughing with his tractor. He had ploughed one strip. Lang drove up the farm lane, walked into the field and waved him down, as if he had papers to sign. He did have papers with him: crop insurance forms. When Amos climbed down, Lang struck him on the head with a heavy wrench from the tractor's toolbox. Afterwards he dropped the wrench into the old well at the edge of the field. Its place in the toolbox has been empty ever since (chapter 8), and the sheriff finds the wrench in the well at the end (chapter 58). He laid him in front of the big rear wheel, put the tractor in gear and let it roll over him. The tractor stopped against the fence with its engine still running. The crushing injuries hid the blow.

Lang left the engine running on purpose. A tractor found with its engine still running looks as if the accident has only just happened. Lang wanted everyone to believe that Amos died in the afternoon, shortly before he was found.

As Lang drove away down the lane, a car came towards him. The lane is two miles long and leads only to the farm. Nobody ever drives on it at that hour. Lang did not see who the driver was, but he knew that somebody had now seen a car leaving the farm at dawn.

So he made a clever decision. He would not hide the visit. He would tell everybody about it himself: "I saw Mr. Hale at first light on the day he died. I took him his crop insurance forms to sign, on my way to court. He was perfectly well when I left him." He never says where on the farm he saw him. (A test reader worked out the hour of the death from the words "He was on his tractor", so they are out. Chapter 4 has the sentence as it is written.) A visit that the lawyer mentions openly looks innocent. And if Amos died in the afternoon, a visit at dawn means nothing.

Then Lang spent the whole day in the courthouse in the county town, in front of a judge and thirty other people. At five o'clock he was called out of the courtroom and told that Amos Hale was dead.

At about four in the afternoon Wade came to the river field and found his father under the tractor with the engine still running. The county coroner is an elected official, the local undertaker, not a doctor. He looked at the body, which had been lying in the sun, and said the accident had happened "some time that afternoon". Everybody assumed that Amos had spent a lazy morning and started the field late, because only one strip was ploughed. The sheriff called it a tragic accident. Old farmers die under their tractors every year.

Lang made one mistake. He did not know that Amos, who counted every drop of diesel, wrote down the tractor's engine hours in his diary every evening.

### B10. Teddy at dawn

The car on the lane was Teddy's.

The evening before, Teddy had begged his father for money in the town tavern, in front of half the county. Amos refused him loudly and called him a drunk. Teddy shouted back: "I'll dance on your grave, old man." Everybody heard it.

At dawn Teddy drove to the farm to try again, before his brother and sister were awake. He had been drinking all night, and his driving licence had been taken away the year before for drunk driving.

He passed a car on the lane and was too nervous to think about it. He found his father dead under the tractor, with the engine running.

He did not call the sheriff, and this is why. He had threatened his father in public a few hours earlier. He was drunk, and he was driving without a licence. He was sure that the first question would be: "What were you doing here?" And all his life, when things go wrong, Teddy runs. He touched nothing. He walked back to his car in his own footprints and drove away. He left the engine running because he was afraid to touch it.

Afterwards, when Lang told everyone that he had seen Amos at dawn and left him well, Teddy understood whose car he had passed. He told himself that the accident must have happened in the few minutes between the lawyer leaving and his own arrival. He did not allow himself to think anything else. If he said a word about that morning, he would have to admit that he left his father lying in a field for ten hours. So he drank more, and he dreamed about the running engine.

So Teddy does not "know that Lang killed his father and do nothing". He does not know. He is afraid to know.

One person saw Teddy that morning, without knowing that it was Teddy. Mrs. Pruitt was at the kitchen window. She saw a figure standing by the tractor, took it for Amos, and called "Mr. Hale?" to it (the prologue). She also saw Teddy's old car come up the lane and go back down a few minutes later. She had also seen Lang's car, a little earlier, but Lang was expected and she thought nothing of it. She loves Teddy like a son, and she knew what he had shouted in the tavern. So she never told the sheriff that he had been there.

### B11. The weeks after the death

The will was filed with the court and Lang was appointed executor. As executor he had to make a list of the dead man's papers. He came out to the farm, went through Amos's desk in the study and made a list of every paper in it. He took the personal letters to his office, including Nora's old letter and her photograph. Then he told Mrs. Pruitt to keep the study locked, so that nothing would go missing (chapter 6). The farm diary stayed in the desk. Nobody thought this strange. From that day the photograph is in Lang's safe.

Lang told the children that their father had made a new will, and that the main beneficiary was a woman called Nora Bell, who had been traced and would come to the reading. He did not read them the will itself, with its words about Carol Bell. That happens in chapter 4, with her in the room. Teddy talked in the tavern, so by the time June arrives the whole county knows that a stranger is named in the will, and many assume she was the old man's woman.

Julian Cross's company then did something practical. It made a private agreement with Colleen and with Teddy. This kind of agreement is called an option. It says: "If you inherit your share of the farm, you promise to sell it to us at this price." The company paid each of them a small fee for signing. Wade refused to sign. Whether Ruth Tanner has signed an option too is still open. So Julian is protected whoever wins. If the granddaughter fails, he buys from the children. If she succeeds, he will try to buy from her.

Lang wrote to "Nora Bell" at the address his investigator had found, and asked her to come.

## Prologue

**When:** Late September, the day Amos died. It is dawn, but the text never says so.

The prologue is two pages, in the first person. The narrator never says who he or she is.

It begins with the narrator already standing in a wet field at dawn, looking at "the old man" lying under his tractor. The engine is still running.

The narrator does not cry and does not scream. The narrator feels sick and cannot stop shaking. The thoughts come in pieces: "I should feel something. What I feel is relief, and that frightens me more than he does." The narrator remembers the sentence the old man said to everyone: "You'll get this land over my dead body." Then comes the thought that decides everything: "Last night I told him what I would do on his grave. Half the county heard me."

The narrator does not touch the body and does not touch the tractor. The narrator walks back to the car in their own footprints and drives away, leaving the engine running.

The prologue never says "I found him dead" and never says "I killed him". It does not mention the car on the lane. The reader finishes it believing that one of the three children killed Amos, or at least stood over him without pity. This is what keeps the reader's suspicion on the children for most of the book. Later we learn that each of the three children had quarrelled with Amos that week, so the voice could belong to any of them.

(The truth: the voice is Teddy's. Everything in the prologue is true. He simply does not say what happened before he arrived. The voice must sound like a frightened, ashamed man who is making a cowardly choice, not like a cold killer. The reader's suspicion comes from what he leaves out.)

The prologue is written (`the-will-prologue.md`). Two details were added there. The narrator says "Dad" aloud, so the voice is one of the three children. And at dawn Mrs. Pruitt calls "Mr. Hale?" from the kitchen door, takes the figure by the tractor for Amos, and goes back in. Draft 4 (15 November) takes every sign of the time of day out of the prologue. It is dawn, but the reader should take it for the afternoon, shortly before Wade "found" him. Until chapter 48 June never says "morning" or "dawn" about the death.

## Part One: "Nora" (about 24 chapters)

### Chapter 1: the bus (written)

**When:** Tuesday 27 October, four weeks after the death. Late afternoon on the bus, dusk in Marlow.

Four weeks after the death, in late October, June is on the last bus of a twelve-hour journey from Milwaukee to Marlow. She has a letter from Everett Lang. It says that a man has died, that the name on the envelope is in his will, and that she should come to his office at ten tomorrow. A bus ticket and a motel room come with it. It does not say how much. She has decided on a few thousand dollars. She is three weeks behind on the rent for a room over a dry cleaner's, and she has given away two shifts to come. She believes free money does not exist.

Sam Tanner sits beside her. He is going to his grandmother Ruth, whose land the data centre wants together with the next farm. The old man next door would not sell, and last month he died under his tractor: Amos Hale. Sam works out that Amos is June's dead man. She tells him the truth: she never heard the name before the letter. He looks at her and decides she was the old man's woman. She does not correct him.

At the gas station Ruth waits in her Buick and stares at June for longer than is polite. June takes it for an old woman warning her off her grandson. At the motel the room is billed to the Estate of Amos Hale. The clerk reads the line, says "So you're the one", and his pen stops.

In her own narration June never says "My name is Nora" and never says "my grandfather". She says "the name on the envelope". Other people call her Nora, and Sam has to say it twice before she moves. This is the rule of the whole book: the narrator may hide things from the reader, but she never tells the reader a false thing. So after the big reveal, a reader who goes back will find that every sentence was true.

This silence has to feel natural, and not like a trick by the author. So it belongs to her character. In prison June learned never to think about the thing she cannot afford to say, because what a person thinks, she will one day say aloud. For six years she has not allowed the name June into her own head. She lives in the present minute and does not look back. The book is written in the present tense for the same reason. When the truth comes out, the reader should feel "how did I miss that?" and not "she was hiding it from me".

### Chapter 2: the diner (written)

**When:** Tuesday 27 October, seven in the evening.

At seven she goes out for food. The grocery is closed, and she will not walk into the tavern alone, so she goes to Lottie's diner. The room goes quiet: the town already knows who she must be. She has forty-one dollars and orders the cheapest thing on the wall. There are not enough tables, so she sits with two old men.

A good-looking stranger asks to join her. He says he is not from here either and gets the same stares, and he makes her laugh. He tells her that a farmer named Hale died under his tractor a month ago. The sheriff wrote down an accident, and nobody in the room believes it. He thinks somebody wanted the old man gone, perhaps his own children. He is here on business. He never asks her one question, not even her name. He pays her bill and leaves.

Pat, the waitress: "That's the data center man." He has been after Amos's farm for a year, and half the room thinks he put Amos under the tractor. The whole room watched her laugh with him. He did not ask her name because he already knew it.

### Chapter 3: Sam at the door, and the coalition (written)

**When:** Wednesday 28 October, ten in the morning.

At ten to ten in the morning Marlow is empty. Sam is waiting at Lang's street door, uninvited. Ruth drove him in and is at the grocery. June notices that of all the seats on that bus he took hers, and now he is at the one door she needs. She decides he is there for the news: whoever gets the Hale farm decides what his grandmother's land is worth. He presses the buzzer himself.

Lang comes down and opens the door himself. He is past sixty, grey all through, and tired. He sends Sam away: the reading is for the family and for the persons named in the will, and Sam is neither. Sam shrugs, says he only wanted to help, and goes.

In the small elevator Lang chews a stomach tablet and makes polite talk that does not interest him: "Did you have a good flight?" He mailed her the bus ticket himself. June still expects a number beside her name, a few thousand dollars, and the next bus north. Nobody has told her what the farm is worth.

From the hallway they hear Colleen and Wade shouting. She asks whether he means to hit her, "or would you like to kill me too?" Amos promised him the farm for forty years and never put it on paper, and he was the one who found the body, alone, at four in the afternoon. Wade answers that he is the only one who wanted the farm kept, and that Colleen, who had not been home in ten years, came home twice in one week once a stranger offered their father eighty million dollars. June hears the number and still thinks of a few thousand for herself: nobody would miss it. Teddy, light and almost cheerful, says they can be heard in Tolliver. Wade laughs, a big laugh with nothing behind it. So the reader learns who wants what: Wade wants the farm kept, Colleen and Teddy want it sold.

When the door opens, Wade is on his feet and looks as if he has not slept in a month. Colleen is in a chair, perfect except for one bitten thumbnail, and her face goes from fury to ice in a second. Teddy leans by the window, rested and friendly, and gives June the only greeting she gets, a nod. The reader should be able to try the prologue voice on all three, and Wade, who found the body, is the first choice.

Nobody greets June. Colleen looks her up and down. Lang seats her alone at the far end of the table, away from the three Hales. Before Lang has reached his chair, Wade tells him, without looking at her, that it all falls into place: blonde, pretty, and forty years in which their father never had a spare dollar for his children. Lang answers that he has not read one line of the will. Colleen says she disagrees with her brother about nearly everything, but she has eyes. Teddy asks everyone to calm down and hear the will: then the other two can kill each other in the parking lot.

June describes them as a **coalition**: three people who can't agree on anything except her.

Then Wade promises to spend what he has on finding out who she is, where she is from and what she was after, and to see her out of the county. Lang stops with one hand on the back of his chair. Wade looks at her for the first time. The chapter ends: "Let them call me a mistress. I've been called worse by people who left a tip. It's the digging I can't afford."

So the mistress idea, which reached the children from the county, does not frighten June. The investigation does.

### Chapter 4: the reading (written)

**When:** Wednesday 28 October, from ten to about half past ten.

Lang does not answer Wade. He reads the will. Everything goes to "the living child of my daughter, Carol Bell", on condition that she lives on the farm for a period exceeding one year.

June came for a few thousand dollars and has been handed the farm that a company wants to buy for $80 million. Lang says it aloud: "Miss Bell was not your father's mistress. She is his granddaughter." This is the first time June hears it. She understands that the real Nora was this man's granddaughter and never said a word about it. To the reader she says only: "Nobody ever told me any of this."

The children are shocked, but less than June expected. They look like people who once heard a story about their father and did not want to believe it. They ask for ten minutes alone, and Lang waits with June on a bench in the hallway. He tells her that he is not her lawyer. The court made him executor, his job is to carry out the will, and he can advise nobody. The eighty million is not in the will. It is the company's offer. June asks how he found her: he hired an investigator, and it took three weeks. He asks why she never came to see her grandfather. She invents two visits, and she tells the reader that it is a lie and why she told it.

She asks whether the children can say that Amos was crazy. Lang answers with a sentence that will matter enormously later: "I saw Mr. Hale at first light on the day he died. I took him his crop insurance forms to sign, on my way to court. His mind was perfectly clear, and he was perfectly rude." He was in court all day, and at five he was called out to be told the news. It sounds like a lawyer defending a will. He says it only to June.

Back in the room Wade speaks for all three: "We want a DNA test. If she's really Dad's granddaughter, let her prove it." Lang does not answer. The chapter ends on June. For one minute she had eighty million dollars. She did not care whether she was really the granddaughter. One test will take it all away. "There is no free money."

So from his first scene Lang is neither kind nor cruel to her. The reader has no reason to look at him twice.

Decided on 15 November: the reader is allowed to suspect that she may not be Amos's granddaughter. The reader must not suspect that she is not Nora Bell. See `test-readers.md`, Problem D.

### Chapter 5: the form, and the locked door (draft 3)

**When:** Wednesday 28 October, from half past ten to noon.

Lang says he cannot ask for the test, because he is the executor. But the children can: a lawyer, the judge in Tolliver, weeks or months. He will not stand in their way. Wade says the hospital in Tolliver still has tissue from his father's operation. Lang adds that he advised Amos against this will, and that it was signed on 14 May. Colleen goes quiet at "May".

Then Lang needs June's decision. He reads one more line of the will: the year begins on the day the will is read. So she sleeps in the house tonight. And he puts a form in front of her, with his pen on it: one signature, and the farm goes to the children. He mocks her job, he says that in the children's place he would pay a man to count her nights, and he says he could close the file by Christmas. He looks like a lawyer who sides with the family and wants her gone.

June thinks it through. Nothing waits for her in Milwaukee. Here she has a free roof and free food. She expects the test to say no: free money does not exist, so somebody has made a mistake. "A man was paid to find a woman, and he found one." Her plan is to live in the house, and to leave on the day a judge orders a test, because a "no" would start people digging. She is also afraid: if somebody killed Amos for this farm, it may be one of the three. She pushes the form back. Wade: "I can see that porch from my kitchen. So I'll know if your light is on."

Lang gives her a copy of the will and tells her which bus to take. He says openly that he will telephone Mrs. Pruitt, the housekeeper, and that the will keeps her in the house.

Outside, Sam has been watching the door from the window of Lottie's. He drives her out in his grandmother's Buick and works out from her suitcase that she got the farm. Before the will, two of the three Hales wanted to sell, and his grandmother only had to wait. Now he needs to know what this stranger will do: "Would you sell it?" "Ask me in a year." He tells her how the town knew her: the Hales were told weeks ago that a woman from Milwaukee would be at the reading, and Teddy told the tavern. He names the data center man: Julian Cross. On the lane he points to the river field, with its one plowed strip. She sends him away in the yard.

The door opens three inches, on a new chain. Half a face and one eye look her over, from her shoes to her dark roots, and then her face for a long time. "Mr. Lang telephoned. He says I'm to give you a bed. He didn't say tonight." The door shuts and the lock turns. June must sleep in that house tonight or lose the farm, and she is locked out.

Mrs. Pruitt's line "Mr. Lang is wrong" was in drafts 1 and 2. It made a test reader name Lang as the villain, and it is kept for chapter 31. Teddy's line about vultures has also left this chapter. The natural place for it is his first visit to the kitchen.

### Chapter 6: the house (written, draft 4)

**When:** Wednesday 28 October, from a little before noon to early afternoon.

June is locked out on the porch, and she must sleep in the house tonight. She puts on her reading glasses and takes out her copy of the will. On page six there are two paragraphs, one under the other. The first says that Agnes Pruitt keeps her room and her wages "for as long as she keeps the said house". The second says that the heir "shall have a room in the said house, and shall sleep in it from the first night". So the hurry was never Lang's: Amos wrote it down himself. Mrs. Pruitt is standing behind the locked door, so June reads both paragraphs aloud to her through it. Then she says it in plain words: the same paper gives both of them their rooms, and a housekeeper who locks the heir out is breaking the will. Then Mr. Lang does not have to pay her or keep her. June tells the reader she has no idea whether that is the law. Mrs. Pruitt does not telephone Lang to ask, because Lang told her to give June a bed. The door opens.

Draft 4 added two things here. On the porch June takes out her phone, a cheap prepaid one, and finds that there is no signal on the farm. So the black telephone in the hallway, which Mrs. Pruitt answers, is the only way to reach anybody outside. And when the door opens and June sees the housekeeper's eyes, she understands the mistake she made in the morning. She told Lang that she had visited the farm twice as a child. This woman has kept the house for forty years and knows that no girl ever came. "All the lawyer has to do is ask her."

Mrs. Pruitt is close to eighty, very small and thin, in a gray dress, a white apron and a gray cardigan buttoned to the neck. Her knuckles are swollen and her hands shake. Her eyes are pale and do not blink. A ring of keys clinks in her apron pocket. She cannot lift June's suitcase, and she climbs the stairs one step at a time. Her body is weak and her mind is sharp.

There is no friendly tour. June sees the house in the order she walks through it, and each thing in it is a thing the book uses later.

- **The hallway.** The front door opens into a narrow, dark, cold hallway, with the staircase straight ahead. Amos's barn coat still hangs on the hooks by the door. A black telephone stands on a little table under the stairs, and Mrs. Pruitt answers it.
- **The living room** is on the right. Nobody has used it "since Mrs. Hale", and every piece of furniture is under a white sheet.
- **The study** is on the left. It has the only lock inside the house. "It is locked, and it stays locked." Mrs. Pruitt carries the one key.
- **The stairs.** There are thirteen steps, and the ninth cracks like a gunshot. Amos would never have it mended, because he liked to know who was on his stairs at night. Mrs. Pruitt has stepped over it for forty years.
- **The bedrooms** are upstairs. None of them has a key. Amos took the keys away when the children were small.

June's room is the last one, over the study. It was Mrs. Hale's. Amos moved his wife into this narrow room when she took sick, "so she wouldn't keep him awake", and she died in that bed on 8 February, forty-one years ago. Nobody has slept in it since. Mrs. Pruitt came to the house in the spring. June reads the choice of bed as an insult: the name in the will is Bell, and the three Hales had never heard of Carol (Teddy asked "Dad had another daughter?"), so Carol did not grow up in this house and her mother was not Mrs. Hale, and the housekeeper has put "the other woman's family" in the wife's bed.

In the floor of the room, between the bed and the wall, there is a square hole with an iron grate over it, the size of a serving tray. Old farmhouses had such grates to let the heat of a downstairs stove rise into the bedroom. This one is right above the locked study. Through it June can see the top of a stove, a piece of rug and one corner of Amos's desk, which is bare. It is rusted open. "Mr. Hale could hear every word that was said in this room." So June can look into the locked room but cannot get into it, and anybody in that room can hear her. She wonders whether she talks in her sleep.

Mrs. Pruitt gives her rules, none of them in the will: supper at six, breakfast at six, and the chain goes on the front door at dark. June answers "Yes, Mrs. Pruitt" to all of it. She has worked in restaurants, and she has seen a cook spit in a rude customer's soup. Mrs. Pruitt never calls her by any name. When Mrs. Pruitt goes downstairs, the ninth step makes no sound.

**The one thing the reader learns in this chapter.** June pushes her suitcase under the bed and it hits another one. It is old, brown and packed: two dresses, a nightgown, a hairbrush, and one pair of a little boy's shoes. In the pocket of the lid is a bus ticket from Marlow to Indianapolis, one way, never used, dated 9 February, forty-one years ago. Mrs. Hale packed a bag to leave this house on the ninth of February, and on the eighth she died in this bed. The chapter ends there.

(The truth is in B1a of the back story. Mrs. Hale was leaving Amos and taking Teddy. She had an infected tooth, and Amos would not pay a dentist or, once he heard about the ticket, send for a doctor. Mrs. Pruitt has known about the suitcase for forty years and gives June that room on purpose. She also knows from the photograph that this woman is not the girl who wrote to Mr. Hale, and she will tell Lang so within the week.)

### Chapter 7: the supper (written, draft 2)

**When:** Wednesday 28 October, six in the evening.

June comes down to supper and steps over the ninth step. Mrs. Pruitt hears her anyway. The kitchen is at the back of the house and is the only warm room. By the back door hangs a feed-store calendar with a pencil. Colleen has telephoned, and Mrs. Pruitt is to mark every night that June sleeps in the house, and every night she does not. So this is the rule of the year: the days are June's, but every night she must be in the house before the chain goes on, and a missed night goes to a judge. Mrs. Pruitt says: "I write down what is so. I always have." June decides to keep a count of her own.

The cellar door is in the corner of the kitchen, and Mrs. Pruitt keeps it shut with an iron hook. The stairs are as steep as a ladder and have no rail. She goes down them quickly, without a light, for a bowl of pickled beets. "Forty years on those stairs. I know every one of them. You don't. They would kill a person who didn't know them. You stay out of my cellar."

**The one thing the reader learns in this chapter.** While Mrs. Pruitt is in the cellar, June stands at the window over the sink. It looks straight at the river field, the plowed strip and the fence. From there you would see a person, but not a face. June asks whether Mrs. Pruitt saw what happened. Mrs. Pruitt shuts the curtain and says: "Mr. Wade found him. I told the sheriff I saw nothing." June notices that she did not say "I saw nothing." So the housekeeper saw something in that field on the day Amos died, and she kept it from the sheriff. The reader remembers the old woman who called "Mr. Hale?" in the prologue, and believes she is protecting whoever stood by the tractor.

(The truth: she saw Lang's car, which was expected, and thought nothing of it. Then she saw Teddy's car on the lane and a figure by the tractor, which she took for Amos. She has kept quiet for love of Teddy.)

Supper is chicken and dumplings from one pot. Mrs. Pruitt does not eat with her. June asks herself what stops this woman from poisoning her, far from town, with the farm going to the Hales if she is gone. Her answer: a second death in this house within a month could not be written down as an accident, and Mrs. Pruitt is old but not stupid. And June has not eaten since last night. She eats. Mrs. Pruitt asks whether she found everything she needs in her room. June understands that the housekeeper wanted her to find the suitcase, and says only: "Everything."

Then she sees a second place laid at the head of the table, in front of the only chair with arms: a plate, a knife, a fork, a glass and an ironed napkin. "Mr. Hale sat down to his supper at six o'clock for sixty years." June says that Sam Tanner told her Mrs. Pruitt did not like him. "I kept Mr. Hale's house for forty years. Nobody asked me to like him." Why lay his place, then? "Because it is his place." June works it out in plain words: the plate is not laid out of love, so it is not laid for the dead man. It is laid for her. It is a show, to tell her whose house this is and to frighten her off the farm, and the bed upstairs said the same thing. She says that Mr. Hale is dead. Mrs. Pruitt straightens his fork and names both of the dead: he died in the field June was just looking at, and Mrs. Hale died in the bed June will sleep in tonight. "This was their house. It still is." Then she ends the chapter: "Do you believe the dead know who is sleeping in their beds?"

(The truth: the plate is the first of Mrs. Pruitt's tricks to drive June out of the house. The pipe smoke, the glasses and the suitcase follow, and she admits all of them in chapter 18.)

The line "You are not how I expected" was in the old plan for this chapter and is out. It told the reader that the housekeeper has a picture of the heir in her head.

### Chapter 8: the first night, and Wade (planned on 16 November)

**When:** The night of Wednesday 28 October, and Thursday 29 October.

The first night is not quiet. June puts her glasses on the table by the bed and leaves the lamp on, so that Wade can see it from his house. In the night she wakes and sees bars of light on her ceiling. The light comes up through the grate: a lamp is burning in the locked study under her. She smells pipe smoke, and only Amos smoked a pipe. She hears slow steps and a pen. Then the light goes out and a key turns. In the morning the study door is locked. Through the grate she sees a pipe lying on the corner of the desk, which was bare the day before. Mrs. Pruitt says only: "Then you heard Mr. Hale."

(The truth: Mrs. Pruitt sat in the study. She wrote the day's line in the farm diary, in Amos's handwriting, as she has done for years: "The girl came. She is not who she says." She burned his tobacco so that June would smell it.)

From her window June sees the river field and, at the bottom of it, the old well with boards across it. Then she meets Wade at the barn.

**The one thing the reader learns in this chapter.** Wade tells her how he found his father: alone, at four in the afternoon, under the wheel of the tractor, with the engine still running. He shows her the tractor, "the one that killed him". The reader hears for the first time how the death looked, from the man who looks most guilty. The running engine is the false clue that makes everybody believe Amos died in the afternoon.

In the tractor's toolbox every tool has its place, and the place of the biggest wrench is empty. Wade says his father never lost a tool in his life. (The truth: Lang hit Amos with that wrench and dropped it into the old well. The sheriff finds it there at the end of the book. It is the one physical proof of the blow.)

Wade also answers two questions the test readers asked. An old man ends up under his own wheel like this: he gets down to clear the plow and starts the tractor from the ground, in gear. And Wade has not plowed the river field since, because he will not drive over the strip where he found his father.

Wade gives her the rules of the farm, as insults. These rules explain who can come and go for the rest of the year.

- The farm belongs to the estate until her year is complete. She is a resident, not yet the owner.
- She cannot dismiss Mrs. Pruitt. The will protects the housekeeper's place for as long as she keeps the house.
- She cannot dismiss Wade. He is employed by the estate as farm manager. For the first time in his life he receives a real salary. He lives in his own small house at the far end of the farm and is in the fields and barns every day.
- The farm's income (crops and cattle) goes into the estate's bank account. Out of it Lang pays the costs, the taxes, Wade's salary, Mrs. Pruitt's wages and June's allowance. What is left waits for whoever finally inherits.
- Colleen comes from the city only for meetings and court. Teddy has no right to be on the farm, but he grew up there, Mrs. Pruitt loves him like a son, and he visits her kitchen often.

So June lives in a house run by a woman she cannot send away, on a farm run by a man she cannot send away, with 1,200 acres that nobody can lock. Anyone could be walking about at night.

Wade ends with one sentence: "You won't see the next harvest." This year's fields are already cut, so he means next fall, before her year is complete.

The chapter ends back in her room. Her reading glasses are gone from the table by the bed. She kneels at the grate and sees them on Amos's desk, in the locked room. (The truth: Mrs. Pruitt climbed the stairs and took them while June was at the barn.)

### Chapters 9–18: the haunting, and the people she suspects

**When:** Early November to mid-December.

**These chapters must be planned again before they are written** (16 November). The study is now locked, and June sees into it through the grate in her floor. Two of the events below, the pipe smoke and the glasses, already happen in chapter 8. The old chapter 8 is therefore no longer a chapter of its own. Still to be placed: Mrs. Pruitt refuses to open the study; June asks for the key and gets it (see part 3, Part E, choice 16); what she reads in the diary and when (the engine hours that Amos wrote every evening, the payments to "D.B.", and the line dated the night she arrived); and Teddy's first visit to the kitchen. The number of chapters will grow, and the later chapters will be renumbered then.

Strange things begin. They come slowly, about one in each chapter, mixed with the other events below.

**In the house:**

- (The reading glasses and the pipe smoke are now in chapter 8.)
- One morning her suitcase stands by the front door, packed, with her clothes folded inside. A week later it happens again.
- In Amos's farm diary a new line appears, in his handwriting, dated after his death: "The girl came. She is not who she says."

**In the fields:**

- At three in the morning she hears the tractor engine in the river field, the field where Amos died. From her window she sees its lights burning. Nobody is in the seat. After ten minutes it stops. In the morning it is cold and silent.

June does not really believe in ghosts. She believes the children are trying to frighten her away, because if she leaves before her year is complete, the farm is theirs.

She answers like a bookkeeper. Ghosts do not burn diesel. She starts keeping Amos's diary herself. Every morning she writes down the tractor's fuel level and the number on its hour meter. An hour meter is a small counter on a tractor that counts the hours the engine has run, like the distance counter in a car. After each "ghost" night the fuel is lower and the hours are higher. A living person is starting that engine.

Reading the old pages of the diary, she also notices small monthly payments many years ago to "D.B." She does not know yet what they mean.

**Suspect: Wade.** One night June hears a noise in the field and finds Wade at the tractor at two in the morning, with the engine cover open and a torch in his hand. She is sure she has caught the ghost, and she says so. Wade is furious, and he has reason. He found his father's body under that machine. He says: "You think I would play ghost with the tractor that killed my father?" He has heard the engine at night too. He believes it is village boys, or June herself, and he came to disconnect the battery. He also tells her something that will matter at the end: "I park her in gear every night, because the brake has been useless for twenty years, and my father joined the wires around the safety switch to save forty dollars. Every morning after one of these nights I find her out of gear. Somebody is touching her. If that fool ever forgets and starts her in gear, she'll walk straight over him."

June does not believe him. But a few nights later the engine runs again while Wade is away overnight at a cattle sale. It was not Wade.

**Suspect: Mrs. Pruitt.** June stays awake and catches Mrs. Pruitt at Amos's desk, writing in the diary in Amos's handwriting. Mrs. Pruitt does not deny it. She explains that she wrote the diary for Amos for years because of his eyes, so the handwriting in it has long been hers. She admits the glasses, the pipe smoke and the suitcase. June asks her why. She answers: "Because I want you out of this house. I have kept it for forty years, and I know who belongs in it." She adds: "I know what I know. The doctors will say what you are soon enough." She means the blood test. She is sure it will expose June, so she sees no need to say more. She swears she has never touched the tractor. She cannot even drive.

So the tricks in the house are explained, and the reason is strange and worrying. The tractor is still not explained.

**Suspect: Julian.** Julian appears again and again, each time in a different way.

- The second time the tractor runs at night, June goes out to the field. A truck is standing on the county road beside the fence with its lights off. It is Julian. He says his company has rented the house next door for him and he could not sleep. (Open: where this house stands. Chapters 1 and 5 say there is nothing on the river road but Ruth's place and Hale Lane. One possibility: it is an old tenant house on Ruth's land.)
- He takes her to dinner in Vincennes, the next town. He is the first person in six years who asks about her life, and she lies to him easily and hates herself for it. She tells him about the tractor. He laughs and says anyone can do that: "You can buy a remote starter for sixty dollars. I have one on my truck." He takes a small remote control from his pocket, presses it, and through the restaurant window she watches his empty truck start by itself. He has just shown her exactly how the ghost works, and he owns the tool.
- He tells her, smiling, what Amos said to him: "He told me I would get his land over his dead body. I told him I could wait."
- She spends a night with him. (Decided on 17 November: by then June knows the house. She slips out after the chain is on the door, Julian is waiting for her, and she is back before morning. Mrs. Pruitt does not know, so no night is missed on the calendar. The way out and in is chosen when the chapter is planned; the outside cellar doors are the natural one.) In the morning, while he showers, she sees a folder in his bag. It contains maps of the Hale farm with buildings drawn on them, dated before Amos died. It contains a page about her: "N. Bell. No family. No savings. Rents a room. Waitress. Will accept a low offer." And it contains a copy of a letter from the company to Amos, two weeks before his death: "We will pursue other avenues." She leaves without waking him.
- A few days later she sees Julian in a café in the county town with Colleen. There are papers on the table between them, and Colleen is signing.

By now the reader is fairly sure that Julian and Colleen are working together, and perhaps that the company had something to do with Amos's death.

(The truth: the maps and the note about her are ordinary business research. "Other avenues" meant other land. The papers in the café were Colleen's option. Julian is hard and practical, but he has done nothing illegal.)

**The kind one: Teddy.** During these weeks Teddy keeps being kind. He brings food. He shows her how the old heating works. He tells funny stories about his father and warns her about Wade's temper. He says: "Dad always swore he would never leave this place. Wade thinks he hasn't." He asks her for nothing. A reader who knows this kind of book will suspect him exactly because he is so nice. That is fine.

**The neighbour: Ruth.** Ruth Tanner stared at June from her Buick on the first evening and has heard Sam's version. She invites June for coffee and tells her the old story: the pretty hired girl, Dot Bell, who left suddenly one summer, and what everybody whispered. June now understands the payments to "D.B." Ruth also says, laughing: "Amos counted every drop of diesel. He wrote down the hours on that tractor every night of his life, like a miser counting his coins."

**June's past.** In one of these chapters, on a night when she cannot sleep, June tells the reader that she has been in prison. She tells the whole story of the son-in-law and the false invoices, and says she was innocent. The reader now believes this is her secret: she is hiding a prison record from the family.

**The detective.** Colleen announces that she has paid a private detective to look into "Nora Bell", and that he found nothing: no husband, no criminal record, not even a parking fine. June lets out a breath. A careful reader should stop here and wonder how a detective could miss three years in prison. This is an honest clue. He found no prison record because he investigated Nora, and it was June who went to prison.

### Chapter 19: the court and the test

**When:** Mid-December.

Colleen's lawyer asks the probate court to order a test, and the judge agrees. Lang says only: "The executor takes no position."

In chapter 5 June planned to leave on the day a judge ordered a test. She cannot: a court nurse is waiting in the next room, and the sample is taken at once.

The test is done properly, by the court. A nurse appointed by the court takes the sample in the courthouse. She rubs a cotton stick on the inside of June's cheek. It is the same idea as spitting in a tube for a company like 23andMe, but an official takes the sample herself, so that nobody can send in another person's saliva. A small piece of tissue from Amos's gallbladder operation, which the hospital in Tolliver still keeps (Wade says so in chapter 5), is sent as well. The laboratory will send its answer in a sealed envelope directly to the judge.

Lang has nothing to do with any of this in public. In secret, he knows which laboratory the court always uses. He has paid a man who works there to make sure the answer is yes. He does this because he believes the true answer would be no, and a "no" would give the farm to the children and end his plan. Neither June nor the reader sees his hand.

### Chapters 20–22: the wait, and Teddy

**When:** Late December.

The answer takes two weeks. For June they are like waiting for a prison sentence. She expects the paper to say she is no relation, and she expects the children to start digging. She thinks about running every night and does not go. Part of her has begun to love the place.

By now she wears Amos's old barn coat every day, because it is the warmest thing in the house.

During these two weeks she catches the tractor ghost. She waits in the dark by the river field and finds Teddy among the trees, with a small remote starter in his hand.

He breaks down and explains. He owes money to dangerous people. He has signed an option with Julian's company and already spent the fee. If June leaves, the farm goes to the three children and his troubles are over. He did not have the courage to hurt anyone, so he tried to frighten her. Every evening after dark he walked out, took the tractor out of gear, switched on its lights and went back to the trees. In the night he pressed the button.

June notices one more thing and says it to the reader: "He has made it look exactly like the morning his father died: the lights on, the engine running, the empty seat. I do not ask him how he knows what that morning looked like. I should have asked."

Then Teddy says: "Mrs. Pruitt told me you are not her. She saw the real girl's photograph years ago."

June's blood runs cold, because it is true. The reader takes it for an old woman's spite.

June could report Teddy to the sheriff, but she does not. She cannot afford a public quarrel in which Teddy repeats that sentence, and she pities him. They make a silent bargain: she will say nothing about him, and he will stop. She holds out her hand, and he puts the remote in it. She says: "If that engine starts again, I will know where to send the sheriff." She drops the remote into the pocket of Amos's coat. The small receiver that Teddy wired into the tractor stays where it is. Nobody thinks of removing it.

### Chapters 23–24: the result

**When:** Early January.

The judge calls everyone to the courthouse and opens the envelope. The laboratory says that the woman known as Nora Bell is the biological granddaughter of Amos Hale.

Here is what each person thinks.

**Colleen** is beaten. She can no longer attack the will, and she has debts she cannot pay.

**Wade** says nothing and walks out.

**Teddy** is confused, because Mrs. Pruitt told him the opposite.

**Lang** is dry and correct. He says to the children: "I am sorry. I know this is not what you hoped for." To June he gives a short nod: "The allowance continues, Miss Bell." Inside, he is satisfied. He believes his money bought this answer.

**Mrs. Pruitt**, when she hears, drops the dish she is holding and says: "Then somebody has lied to the judge."

**June** is the most frightened person in the building. She will explain why on the next page.

The last lines of Part One are:

"The paper says I am Amos Hale's granddaughter. I should be the happiest woman in the county. I have never been so frightened in my life."

At this moment the reader still does not know her secret and does not understand her fear. The explanation comes one page later.

## Part Two: "June" (about 16 chapters)

### Chapter 25: she tells the reader the truth

**When:** Early January, the night of the result.

Part Two opens with: "My name is not Nora Bell."

June now tells the reader everything she has hidden: her real name, the trade with Nora, the death certificate, the six years. She says that Nora spoke a few words at the very end, and that she took them for kindness. She does not repeat the words here. The reader hears them only on the last pages of the book.

She also explains why she is certain the paper is wrong. The lawyers did not find her. They found Nora. They traced Nora's birth certificate and Nora's mother, Carol. June is a different woman who borrowed Nora's name. As far as she knows she met Nora by chance, as a stranger renting a room. So the paper cannot be true, and that means somebody has falsified it.

Then she explains her fear. Somebody was able to reach inside a laboratory used by a court. That somebody must know she is an impostor, because there is no other reason to falsify the test. And that somebody wants her to stay on this farm. She does not know who it is or why. Perhaps he wants her to inherit so that he can take the farm from her afterwards. Perhaps he wants her here so that something can happen to her at a moment he chooses. Someone she cannot see is moving her like a piece in a game.

The reader now reads Part One again in memory. Her fear of the test was not doubt. The detective found no prison record because he looked at the wrong woman. Mrs. Pruitt's line in the diary was simply true.

One door stays open, and it is an honest one. June is adopted and does not know who her birth family is. A sharp reader may wonder. June never wonders, because to her Nora was a stranger she met by accident.

### Chapters 26–30: who falsified the test?

**When:** January.

It was not the children. They wanted her to fail.

She thinks of Julian. He has money, and he has spent weeks charming a poor waitress. One grateful owner would be the cheapest seller he could wish for. She goes to him and accuses him.

Julian does not get angry. He opens his bag and shows her the options signed by Colleen and Teddy. He explains his position. If she had failed the test, the farm would have gone to the three children. Two of them have already promised to sell to him. Wade refuses, but Wade would own only one third. When the owners of a property cannot agree, any one of them can ask a court to order the whole property sold and the money divided. The company's lawyers say that such a case takes less than a year and cannot be lost. With June as the only owner, Julian has nothing but hope, because she may never sell. He says: "With your aunt and your uncle I had two signatures and a court case I could not lose. With you I have a hope. Why would I commit a crime to exchange a certainty for a hope?"

It is logical, and she has to believe him. Then he says something she does not expect: "I am guilty of plenty where you are concerned. I came to that diner on purpose. I took you to dinner on purpose. I am not guilty of that test." He adds, a little hurt: "You think I am the wolf because I am the only one here who says openly that he wants your land." They part coldly.

Now June has nobody left to suspect. The person who falsified the test has no face.

### Chapters 31–34: Mrs. Pruitt

**When:** Late January, from a Friday night to the Sunday.

Mrs. Pruitt comes to June's room at night and at last says everything:

"Seven years ago a girl wrote to Mr. Hale and put her photograph in the envelope. I read him the letter. I held that photograph in my hand. She was a dark girl with a mole on her cheek and a crooked front tooth. You could be her cousin. You are not her. You told the Tanner boy you never heard Mr. Hale's name. The girl who wrote that letter knew it well enough. I told Mr. Lang so in the very first week. I would not go to Miss Colleen, not after what she tried to do to her father. Mr. Lang said a housekeeper's memory is not evidence. He said that if I accused the heir without proof I could lose my place in this house, and that the test would settle it. So I waited for the test. Well, the test has settled nothing, and the photograph has gone from Mr. Hale's desk. I have kept one secret too many in this house already. I saw who came up the lane on the morning Mr. Hale died, and I held my tongue for love. I will not hold it for you. On Monday I am going to Miss Colleen's lawyer in the city. Let him take your fingerprints, and we shall see who you are."

(The line "Mr. Lang is wrong", which was cut from the end of chapter 5, can come back in this speech.)

June understands that this is the end. Fingerprints would lead straight to the prison records of June Mercer. She begs Mrs. Pruitt to wait. They quarrel loudly. Teddy, who is visiting the kitchen downstairs, hears them shouting. He hears every word, including the words about the lane. June does not understand those words. She thinks the old woman is talking about some old family quarrel.

The reader, and June, do not see what happens next. On Saturday Mrs. Pruitt telephones Lang. She demands the photograph, which she believes is among the estate papers, and she tells him she is going to Colleen's lawyer on Monday.

For Lang this is a disaster. If Colleen's lawyer takes fingerprints, June is exposed, the farm goes to the children, and his $76 million is gone. An investigation might also reach the man he paid at the laboratory.

Everyone knows that Lang spends the last weekend of every month at his fishing cabin, four hours' drive to the north. He is seen there on Saturday afternoon and again on Sunday morning. In between, he drives back through the night, comes into the cellar through the two slanted wooden doors behind the house, because the front door is on its chain. The door from the cellar into the kitchen is held shut by Mrs. Pruitt's iron hook, on the kitchen side. She hears somebody in her cellar, lifts the hook herself and stands on the top step. She says his name, as if he had come to help her, and he pulls her down the steep stairs. (The cellar doors were decided on 16 November. The hook is in chapter 7. That she opens the door herself is proposed on 17 November: it explains why June finds the cellar door standing open.)

On Sunday morning June comes down to the kitchen and finds her dead at the bottom of the stairs.

### Chapters 35–37: the sheriff

**When:** The end of January and the first days of February.

The sheriff comes. An old woman has fallen on a steep stair, and that may be an accident. But Teddy, frightened and honest, tells him about the quarrel he heard. Colleen arrives from the city and screams that the stranger has murdered the one person who doubted her.

The reader has been inside June's head and saw her go to bed and wake up, so the reader does not seriously believe she did it. What matters is that the sheriff and the family suspect her. The reader's own suspicion goes somewhere else. Mrs. Pruitt said she saw who came up the lane on the morning Amos died, and two days later she is dead. The reader remembers the voice in the prologue and thinks: one of the children has killed again. The sheriff tells her politely not to leave the county.

June now sees her position clearly. If the sheriff ever arrests her, even for one night, they will take her fingerprints. The fingerprints will answer "June Mercer, convicted thief, officially dead". Then she will be a woman under a false name, about to inherit a fortune, with a dead housekeeper who had been calling her a fraud. No jury would believe she is innocent.

### Chapters 38–40: "Sit down, June"

**When:** Early February, one evening and the night after it.

June decides to give everything up. If she refuses the inheritance, the farm goes to the children, and nobody will have a reason to look at her any more. She can leave tonight and disappear before the sheriff has grounds to arrest her.

To refuse an inheritance, a person signs a paper called a renunciation and gives it to the executor. It is the same form that Lang put in front of her, with his pen on it, on her first morning (chapter 5). So she goes to Lang's office at closing time. She does not go because she trusts him. She goes because he is the official who holds the form.

She tells him she wants to sign and go. He answers in his dry way: "A renunciation cannot be undone, Miss Bell. I am obliged to tell you to think again." She says she has thought. He takes a form from his drawer and takes the cap off his pen.

Then he puts the cap back on. He gets up, closes the office door and turns the key. He says:

"Sit down, June."

Nobody in this county knows that name. She has never spoken it here. With those two words he tells her that he knows who she really is, that he has always known, and that from this moment she must do what he says.

The rest of the scene is short. Lang does not explain himself. He says only what he needs to say to hold her.

**How he knows.** He lays three photographs on the desk: the dark girl with the mole, taken from an old letter; the waitress photographed by his investigator; and June Mercer's prison photograph.

**Why he is saying it now.** He would have preferred to say nothing until the autumn. But she was about to walk out, and he cannot allow that.

**What she must do.** She will go back to the farm and be Nora Bell until her year is complete and the court has given her the farm. On that same day she will sign it over to a buyer he will name, for $4 million. Then she will take her money and go far away. He does not say who the buyer is. He does not say what he gains.

**What happens if she refuses or runs.** He gives the photographs to the sheriff: "Living under a dead woman's name. Lying to a court. And a dead housekeeper who was telling people you were a fraud. I would not even need to lie."

She asks about the test. He says: "The court asked a laboratory a question. I made sure of the answer."

She asks about Mrs. Pruitt. He says: "A woman of eighty on a steep stair with no rail. On Saturday night I was four hours north of here with a fishing rod, and the sheriff has already checked. This family does its own dying, Miss Mercer. I only keep the books." Then he offers her something: "I will speak to the sheriff for you. The word of an executor counts for something."

He ends: "We understand each other. You have been in this trade longer than I have." He truly believes she is a thief like himself. This belief is the mistake that destroys him at the end.

June says yes.

That night she works out the rest by herself, because she is a bookkeeper. Nobody goes to this much trouble for a lawyer's fee. The buyer must be Lang himself, hidden behind a company. He will pay $4 million for land that one company wants for $80 million, and he will keep the difference. He did not need to tell her.

**What June knows now, and what she does not.** She knows that Lang has known her real name from the beginning, that he bought the laboratory's answer, and that he is using her to take the farm. She does not think he is a killer, and she has good reasons. As far as anyone knows, Amos died in an accident in the afternoon, while Lang was standing in front of a judge. And Lang was four hours away on the night Mrs. Pruitt fell. To June he is a greedy, patient man who waited thirty years for an old farmer to die and then made use of it.

**What the reader thinks.** The reader knows one thing that June does not: the prologue. Someone stood over Amos's body and walked away, and that someone was one of his children. Then Mrs. Pruitt died, two days after saying she had seen who came up the lane that morning. So the reader believes there is a killer inside the Hale family, and that Lang is a different kind of villain: a man who lets other people do the dying and collects afterwards. "This family does its own dying" seems to confirm it. For the next ten chapters the book lets the reader believe this.

**Why the book is not finished here.** The question "who falsified the test?" is answered, but three bigger questions are open. Which of the children stood over Amos, and did that person also kill Mrs. Pruitt? What will happen to June in the hands of a man like Lang? And what will June herself do: will the woman who was punished for a theft she did not commit now become a real thief?

## Part Three: "The Bargain" (about 12 chapters)

### Chapters 41–43: she accepts

**When:** February and March.

June does what most people would do. She accepts the deal, and she does not investigate anything.

She is even relieved. The person in the shadows has a face, and what he wants is money. Four million dollars is more than she ever imagined. Lang keeps his word about the sheriff, and Mrs. Pruitt's death is recorded as an accident.

She tells herself the old stories: the children are rich already; she paid three years of her life for a crime she did not commit; life owes her this. But she knows that this time the accusation would be true. For the first time she is really becoming what the jury said she was. This is the lowest point of her character, and the reader should feel uneasy about her.

One night she reads the estate's accounts, which she has the right to see. She is looking for small thefts, out of habit. She finds none. Every dollar is in its place. That frightens her more than theft would. A man who steals small amounts is content with small amounts. This man has kept a year of perfect books because he is waiting for one single number.

Then Colleen comes to the farm, desperate. She has borrowed half a million dollars, and she offers it to June to leave before the year is up, so that the farm goes to the children. It is exactly the way out June wanted on the night she drove to Lang's office. She telephones Lang, and he says one word: "No." She has to refuse Colleen to her face. Now she understands that she is not his partner. She is his property.

### Chapters 44–46: the farm, and Wade's offer

**When:** Early April (calving) to July.

Winter passes, then spring and summer. There is no housekeeper now, and June has to work. She learns the animals, the machines and the fields beside Wade. By the summer she drives the old tractor herself. Wade stops being cruel and becomes merely rude. She begins to belong somewhere, for the first time in her life.

One evening Wade makes her an honest offer. He explains his position, and it is about money as well as love.

If she fails or leaves, the farm goes to the three children. Colleen and Teddy want to sell, and a court would order the whole farm sold. Wade would receive about $26 million and lose the only thing he has ever done. He says: "I would be a rich man with nowhere to stand."

If she sells to the data centre, the farm is gone as well.

So he offers a third way: "When it is yours, do not sell. Rent me the land. I will pay you rent every year for the rest of your life, more than you have ever seen, and you will never have to lift a shovel. I will farm it for myself at last, not for my father." For Wade this is real money: the profit of a large farm that he has run for thirty-five years for nothing.

It is the only honest offer anyone has made her, and she cannot accept it, because Lang owns her. She says she will think about it. Lying to Wade is the first thing in the whole bargain that truly hurts.

### Chapter 47: a small worry

**When:** August.

Something will not leave her alone. She asks the bookkeeper's question about Mrs. Pruitt's death: who gained from it? She gained. Lang gained exactly as much. But Lang was four hours away that night, and the sheriff checked. And Mrs. Pruitt's last angry words were about something else: she had seen who came up the lane on the morning Amos died. June does not know what that means. The question stays with her, without an answer.

### Chapters 48–49: the numbers

**When:** Early September.

In late summer, almost a year after the death, Wade is ploughing the river field again. One evening he shows June the sheriff's photographs of his father's accident. He has never been able to accept that death. He tells her what has troubled him all year:

"One strip. He had ploughed one strip of that field when I found him at four in the afternoon. My father was in his fields at first light every day of his life. What was he doing all day?"

June looks at the photographs. In one of them she can read the number on the tractor's hour meter. She reads it only from habit, because numbers are what she reads.

She fetches the diary. The last entry, written the evening before the death, gives the number of engine hours. The number in the sheriff's photograph is eleven hours higher.

She tells Wade what the numbers say, and she is careful to say no more than they say. The sheriff switched the engine off at about four in the afternoon. Eleven hours before that is five in the morning. So the engine was started at first light, as on every other day of Amos's life, and it ran without stopping. But in eleven hours of ploughing he would have finished the field, and he ploughed one strip, which is ten minutes of work. So the tractor worked for ten minutes and then stood with its engine running for almost eleven hours. The eleven hours **exceed** anything that the afternoon story can explain.

Wade adds two things that only a son would know. Nobody else touched that tractor that day. And his father never let an engine run without working, not for one minute, because diesel was money.

So something stopped Amos ten minutes after he began, at dawn, and it stopped him so suddenly that he never reached the key. The numbers cannot say what it was. They say only that the afternoon story is wrong.

Wade does not see where this leads. June does, and her stomach turns over. She remembers a dry voice beside a bench in a hallway, almost a year ago: "I saw Mr. Hale at first light on the day he died." And she remembers what he added: that he was in court for the rest of that day. He said it only to her (chapter 4).

This is not proof, and she knows it. A man may visit a farmer at dawn and leave him alive. But for the first time she looks at Lang's alibi and sees its shape. It covers exactly the hours in which everybody believes Amos died. It does not cover the hour in which the numbers say he fell. And she knows something that Wade does not: she knows what kind of man Lang is, and how much money he is waiting for.

### Chapter 50: Teddy

**When:** Mid-September.

June needs someone who saw that morning. She remembers what she noticed and did not ask on the night she caught Teddy. His "ghost" showed the death scene as it looked at dawn: lights on, engine running, an empty seat. But officially nobody saw the scene before Wade did, at four in the afternoon, in full daylight. Teddy's ghost was not something he invented. It was something he remembered.

She goes to Teddy with the diary and the photograph and says: "You were there."

Teddy breaks. He was the voice in the prologue. He tells her about the quarrel in the tavern, the drinking, the licence, the body, the footprints, and the ten hours he left his father lying in the field. He tells her that Mrs. Pruitt saw his car from the kitchen window and never told anyone. She said to him once: "What a man does in his grief is forgiven."

For a few pages the reader believes that Teddy killed his father, and then killed the old woman who knew he was there. June believes it too, and she is alone in a room with him.

Then Teddy says the thing the prologue left out: "The lane is two miles of nothing. In forty years I never met a car on it before six in the morning. That morning I met one coming out. When Mr. Lang said he had been there early, I thought: so that was him. And I told myself Dad must have fallen right after he left."

Now the pieces fit. Lang was leaving the farm as Teddy arrived, and when Teddy reached the field a few minutes later, Amos was already dead. There was no time for an accident in between.

The numbers and Teddy need each other. Teddy alone is a drunk who threatened his father and ran away, and nobody would believe him. The numbers alone show that something happened at dawn, but not what, and not who was there. Together they show that Lang was with Amos at the minute he died, and that he has hidden it for a year.

Even together they are not proof for a court. Nobody saw the blow. But June is not a court. She is certain.

### Chapter 51: what it means for her

**When:** Mid-September.

June now thinks it through to the end.

Lang killed Amos. He did it just when Amos wanted to travel north to see his granddaughter and have her blood tested, because that visit would have ended the plan. Then he left the engine running, so that the death would look fresh in the afternoon, and he went to sit in front of a judge. His alibi was not luck. He built it.

The fishing cabin is the same kind of alibi. He was seen on Saturday afternoon and on Sunday morning. Nobody saw him in between, and four hours is not far for a man who drives at night. Mrs. Pruitt threatened the plan, and she is dead at the bottom of a staircase.

After the signing, only one living person will know what Lang has done, and that person is June. He will not give $4 million and his freedom to a woman who can destroy him. In his accounts she is not a partner. She is the last cost to be removed.

The $4 million was never real. The day she signs is the day she is meant to die.

This is the answer to the question "why does the murder matter to June?" It matters because the man who holds her on a leash is the murderer, and that turns her comfortable bargain into a death sentence.

### Chapter 52: Wade, and the plan

**When:** Late September.

She cannot fight Lang alone. She tells Wade everything, including the thing that may make him hate her: she is not Nora Bell.

Wade is silent for a long time. He has been lied to for a year. Then he does the arithmetic. If she is exposed, the farm goes to the three children and is sold. If Lang wins, the farm is sold, and his father's murderer becomes rich. The farm survives only if June survives and Lang is caught.

He says: "If you are a fraud, I lose the farm. Until October you are my niece."

They cannot simply go to the sheriff, and June explains why. Her word is worthless: she is a convict living under a false name. Teddy is a drunk who ran from his father's body. The numbers show that the afternoon story is wrong, but not who killed him. The moment she speaks, Lang will hand over his photographs, she will be arrested, and he will say that a criminal is trying to blame the lawyer who found her out. He would walk free. They need Lang's own words, and they need him caught with a weapon in his hand.

June makes the plan, and she builds it on Lang's one mistake. He believes she is a thief. So she will behave like one.

In the book the reader is told only part of the plan at this point: that there will be letters, and that June gives Wade and Teddy each a job. The reader discovers the rest as it happens. For you, the author, here is all of it.

The date is known in advance. The judge will sign the farm over to her ten days after her year ends, and Lang will come that evening with the sale papers.

1. **The letters.** On the last day June will post a full written confession to the sheriff and to Colleen's lawyer, with copies of the diary page and the photograph and a statement signed by Teddy. If she dies, the truth will arrive anyway. By writing it she also gives up the farm, because she believes she has no right to it. This is her real decision. She chooses the truth and prison over the money. It is the opposite of what the son-in-law did, and the opposite of what Lang expects from her.
2. **Wade obeys.** June expects Lang to find a reason to send Wade away on the last night, because he will want her alone. Wade must go, so that Lang suspects nothing. Before he leaves, Wade hides an old telephone in Amos's study, recording.
3. **Teddy waits.** Teddy will leave his car outside the tavern, where it stands every night, so that anyone who looks for him believes he is drinking. He will walk across the fields and hide in the trees by the river field, as he did on the nights of his "ghost". He has two jobs. When he sees June and Lang come into the field, he telephones the sheriff. When June shouts his name, he presses his remote, so that the engine and the lights show Lang that he has been seen, and give June a moment to run. For this the tractor is to stand out of gear, as it did on the ghost nights. (Chapter 6 says June's phone has no signal at the farmhouse. Decided on 17 November: there is a signal under the power line by the river, so Teddy can call from the trees.)
4. **June plays the thief.** After she signs, she will ask Lang for more money and tell him that she knows about the dawn. She will say that the diary page and the photograph are hidden on the farm. Lang will have to see them and take them before she "leaves". She will let him force her to lead him to the hiding place: the toolbox of the tractor in the river field. On the way he will talk, because he believes she will be dead within the hour. A second telephone in the pocket of Amos's coat will record him.

Nobody plans a death. The plan is to get Lang's words, and to have the sheriff find him in that field with a weapon.

June takes the remote out of the pocket of Amos's coat, where it has been for almost a year, and puts it back into Teddy's hand. The whole plan now depends on the family coward.

## Part Four: "Everett" (3 short chapters)

**When:** Early November, about ten days after her year ended on 29 October. Lang tells it on the last evening. (The calendar of 16 November put the last night on 29 October itself. That was a mistake: the judge needs about ten days, as B7 and this part both say.)

For three short chapters the book changes voice. The narrator is Lang, and it is the last day.

This part is placed here, and not earlier, so that the reader does not learn about the murders before June does. Now that she has worked it out, the reader is allowed inside his head, and learns the pieces that June could not know.

June's year ended ten days ago. This afternoon the judge signed the order that gives her the farm, and Lang registered it. She is the owner. Tonight she will sign it over to Hale River Holdings.

While he waits for the evening, Lang remembers.

He remembers the two photographs side by side, and the moment he understood that a false heir was worth $76 million to him.

He remembers the dawn in the river field. The old man climbed down from the tractor because he thought his lawyer had brought papers to sign. Lang remembers the wrench, which he dropped into the old well, the wheel, and the engine left running so that the death would look fresh. He remembers the car on the lane, and his decision to speak about his visit openly. He remembers sitting in court all day, waiting to be called out.

He remembers the man at the laboratory, and what he paid him.

He remembers Mrs. Pruitt on the telephone asking for the photograph. He remembers the night drive back from the cabin, and the cellar stairs.

He thinks about June with contempt. To him she is a thief who was stupid enough to be caught.

Then he goes through tonight, and he is careful, as always. As executor he has ordered Wade to take the young cattle to the autumn sale, two counties away. At eight o'clock he telephones the sale yard himself and is told that Wade's truck is there. On his way through the town he looks at the tavern. Teddy's car is outside, where it has stood every night for twenty years. He has spent part of the afternoon at the old well at the edge of the river field, loosening the boards that cover it. He has a pistol in his coat, for the walk and not for use. Her suitcase will go into his car, and her own car will be left at the bus station in the city. Everyone will say they always knew she would take the money and run.

The part ends as he turns into the lane at dusk: "The lane is empty this time. I looked."

The reader finishes this part afraid. Wade is two counties away. Teddy, it seems, is in the tavern. June is alone.

## Part Five: "June Mercer" (about 5 chapters)

### Chapter 56: the last evening

**When:** The last evening, in early November.

Lang arrives two hours earlier than he said. He takes her telephone "so that we are not disturbed". He does not think to look for a second one. He goes upstairs, and when he comes down, her suitcase is standing packed by the front door, exactly as in the first weeks. This time she knows who packed it, and she knows what it is for.

In Amos's study she signs two papers:

- the sale of the farm to Hale River Holdings for $4 million. It is the first time she sees the buyer's name;
- a short note in her own handwriting, which he dictates: "I have sold the farm and I am leaving. Do not look for me."

After these two signatures he does not need her alive.

Now June does the bravest thing she does in the book. She does not get up. She says that four million is not enough, and that she wants half.

Lang almost laughs. Then she tells him what she has. Teddy passed him on the lane that morning. The tractor ran for eleven hours and ploughed one strip. She has the diary page and the sheriff's photograph, and she has put them where he will not find them.

It is exactly what Lang expects from a thief, so he believes every word. He does not suspect a trap, because in his eyes she is only being greedy. He also cannot let her "leave" tonight while those papers exist. He takes the pistol from his coat and says: "Show me."

She lets him force her. She tells him the papers are in the toolbox of the tractor, in the river field.

Lang is content. The old well is at the edge of that same field. He thinks she is walking to her own grave and believes she is bargaining.

### Chapter 57: the river field

**When:** The last night, in early November.

They walk out in the dark, and Lang talks. He talks because he believes she will be dead within the hour, and because for a year he has had nobody to tell how clever he has been. He says that Teddy is a drunk and nobody will ever believe him. He says of Amos: "He climbed down for me, you know. He thought I had brought him papers." He says of Mrs. Pruitt that she should have learned to hold her tongue. The telephone in the pocket of Amos's coat records all of it.

They reach the tractor at the end of the last row. June looks at the trees by the river. Nothing moves. She cannot tell whether Teddy is there.

The toolbox is fixed beside the driver's seat. June climbs up onto the step. Lang stays on the ground beside the step, with the pistol raised and his torch on her hands. The place where he is standing is directly in front of the big rear wheel.

She opens the toolbox and hands him down the diary page and the photograph. He looks at them and puts them in his pocket. Then he says: "Come down."

She understands that when her feet touch the ground, she dies. And she understands something else. The plan was to frighten him with light and noise, like a ghost. This man is not afraid of ghosts. Light and noise will not stop him. He will simply shoot.

So she does the one thing that nobody planned. Her hand is resting on the gear lever that she has used all summer. She remembers Wade's words about what this tractor does when it is started in gear. She pushes the lever into gear.

Lang reaches up with his free hand to pull her down by the ankle. She shouts: "Teddy! Now!"

Nothing happens. Lang smiles and says: "Teddy is where he is every night."

One second passes, and another.

Then the **engine** starts. The tractor is in gear, and it lurches forward. The wheel takes Lang down. The pistol fires once into the air. He dies in the same field as Amos, in the same way.

The tractor stops against the fence with its engine running. Teddy comes out of the trees. He was there the whole time. He had telephoned the sheriff, as he promised. Then he saw the pistol and could not move, exactly as he could not move a year ago. He says: "Then you called my name." He climbs up and switches the engine off, which is the thing he did not do on the morning his father died. The river field is quiet.

The plan was Teddy's light and noise. The gear was June's alone, chosen in the second when she saw that nothing else would save her. Teddy did not know.

### Chapter 58: the investigation

**When:** The last night, and the three days after it.

The sheriff arrives a few minutes later, because Teddy called him before the engine started. Wade arrives soon after.

June does not hide, and she does not run. Her first words to the sheriff are: "My name is June Mercer." Her next words are: "I put the tractor in gear. Teddy did not know."

A man is dead, so of course the sheriff investigates, and June is the person who caused his death. She is held for three days. This is what the investigation finds.

- Lang's pistol is in his hand, and it has been fired once.
- Her packed suitcase is in the boot of Lang's car. Her telephone is in his pocket. In his briefcase are the two papers she signed, including the goodbye note.
- The boards over the old well, twenty steps from the tractor, have been freshly loosened.
- At the bottom of the well lies the big wrench from the tractor's toolbox. Its place in the toolbox has been empty since chapter 8. It is the one physical proof of the blow that killed Amos.
- The telephone in the coat recorded everything: her demand, his answers, and what he said on the walk about Amos and about Mrs. Pruitt. The telephone that Wade hid in the study recorded the first half as well.
- Teddy saw it all, and his call to the sheriff was made before Lang died.
- On Monday her letters arrive. They carry the post office stamp from the morning before the death. In them she describes Lang's threat and writes: "If I disappear tonight, look in the old well."
- In Lang's office safe are the things he had to keep: the three photographs and the investigator's report, which were his hold over her, and the papers of Hale River Holdings.
- Julian Cross tells the sheriff that a company called Hale River Holdings has been offering his firm the Hale farm since the spring, months before the heir had any right to sell it.
- The diary, the hour meter and Teddy's statement reopen the death of Amos. Mrs. Pruitt's death is reopened as well.

The district attorney decides that June acted in self-defence. An armed man was about to kill her, and his own recorded words prove what he intended. She is not charged for Lang's death. She is charged for living under a false name.

### Chapter 59: the test

**When:** November.

In her statement June repeats what Lang told her about the test: "I made sure of the answer." The judge orders the laboratory to be examined. Before anyone reaches him, a man who works there comes forward with a lawyer of his own. He is terrified, and he wants it known that he changed nothing. He says:

"Mr. Lang paid me to make the test say yes. Before I changed anything, I ran the samples, to see how much I would have to change. I did not have to change anything. It was already yes."

The judge orders a second test at a different laboratory. It gives the same answer. June Mercer is the biological granddaughter of Amos Hale.

June cannot understand it.

The will asks for one thing more: she must be Carol's child. So the judge orders June's closed adoption file to be opened. It names her birth mother: Carol Bell.

Now the law is clear. The will leaves the farm to "the living child of my daughter Carol Bell". It does not name Nora. June is Carol's living child, and she has lived on the farm for more than a year. She inherits it under her own name.

Lang committed two murders to hide a lie that was never a lie. If he had let Amos take his blood test, it would have said yes. And June spent a year believing she was stealing something that was hers.

Colleen tries once more to break the will, and loses. The options that she and Teddy sold to Julian are worth nothing now, because they inherit nothing.

### Chapter 60: the box

**When:** November and December.

For six years June has refused to ask where she comes from. Now a judge has told her the name of her mother, and it is the name of Nora's mother. She takes the small brass key to the bank. The court has put the records right: Nora Bell died six years ago, and June is her closest living relative. The bank opens the box for her.

Inside are her own adoption papers, the old letters of their grandmother Dot, and a letter in Nora's handwriting that begins "Dear June".

Nora tells her everything. She was the younger sister, the one who was kept. She found June on purpose, on the day June left prison. She tried to tell her on the first evening, and June's anger frightened her into silence. She gave June her name out of love, and also because the name Bell was June's by right. She did not have the courage to say it to her face, so she left it here for the day June would want to know.

Only now does the reader hear Nora's last words. June remembers the hospice, and Nora saying: "You were always my sister." June had answered: "You too." She thought they were being kind to each other.

All her life June hated the sister who was kept. That sister spent the last year of her life looking after her and gave her everything she had.

June pleads guilty to living under a false name and to giving that name to a court. These are real offences, and Nora's permission does not change that. But the prosecutor asks the judge for mercy. She came forward by her own choice, at a moment when silence would have made her rich, and her evidence solved two murders and stopped a fraud. The judge gives her probation, which means she stays free under supervision, and community service.

She keeps the farm. She accepts Wade's offer and rents him the land, so that he farms for himself at last. Julian tells her, smiling, that he is paid to wait.

On the last page she signs the court's paper. For the first time in six years she writes her own name: June Mercer.

## PART D — WHAT THE READER BELIEVES, STEP BY STEP

1. **Prologue.** One of the three children killed Amos, or at least left him lying there. (False. It is Teddy, and he only found him.)
2. **Chapters 1–7.** She is a lucky, frightened outsider. The children are her enemies. The lawyer is a grey official who sides with the children. (The first two are true. The third is false.) Also: the Tanners and the data centre man had good reasons to want Amos dead. (True, and neither had any part in it.) From chapter 6: Amos may have let his own wife die, so his children had forty years to hate him. From chapter 7: the housekeeper saw something in that field and is protecting somebody.
3. **Chapters 8–18.** Someone is frightening her away. Wade is caught, and he is innocent. Mrs. Pruitt is caught, and she is guilty of the tricks in the house only. Julian looks guiltier every time he appears, and he is innocent.
4. **Chapters 20–22.** Teddy is the tractor ghost. (True.) Mrs. Pruitt's claim that she is "not the girl" is spite. (False. It is true.)
5. **Chapter 24.** She is the granddaughter, as the paper says, and her fear is strange.
6. **Chapter 25.** She is an impostor with no connection to the family, and somebody falsified the test. (She is not Nora. But she is family, and nobody falsified anything.)
7. **Chapters 26–30.** Julian falsified the test. (False.)
8. **Chapters 31–37.** Mrs. Pruitt knew which of the children was at the farm that morning, and she has been killed for it. (False. She was killed for what she knew about June.)
9. **Chapters 38–40.** The grey lawyer is the one moving June. He is a blackmailer but not a killer: he was in court when Amos died and at his cabin when Mrs. Pruitt died. (Both alibis are true, and both are worthless.)
10. **Chapters 41–47.** June has become a thief at last, and the killer is still one of the children.
11. **Chapters 48–51.** The afternoon story is wrong. For a few pages Teddy did it. Then it is Lang.
12. **Part Four and chapter 56.** The plan has failed: Wade is far away, Teddy is in the tavern, and June is alone. (False. June arranged both.)
13. **Chapter 59.** She is a fraud who gave up everything to tell the truth. (False. She was the true heir all along.)

### Where the reader learns each piece of the back story

- The affair with the hired girl: Ruth Tanner's story, and the "D.B." payments in the diary (Part One).
- June's conviction: June tells it herself (Part One).
- The will and Amos's reasons: the meeting in Lang's office, and what Wade and Ruth say about him.
- The trade of names: June tells it in chapter 25.
- Nora's letter and photograph: Mrs. Pruitt, chapter 31.
- Lang's discovery of who June is: the "Sit down, June" scene. His profit: June's own arithmetic that night.
- The murder of Amos: the numbers, Teddy's confession, Lang's own part, and Lang's words on the last walk.
- Mrs. Pruitt's murder and the money paid at the laboratory: Lang's own part.
- The sisters: the adoption file, and Nora's letter in the bank box.

Nobody ever sits down and explains everything to June. Each piece comes from a different person, and some pieces first arrive in a false form.

## PART E — THE THREE WORDS, AND OPEN CHOICES

### The three words

- **Coalition:** the three children who hate each other unite against the stranger in Lang's office.
- **Engine:** the running tractor engine at Amos's death, in the night haunting, in the numbers that show when he fell, and at Lang's death.
- **Exceed:** the will's condition that she live on the farm for a period exceeding one year; and the eleven engine hours that exceed what the afternoon story can explain.

### Choices still open

1. **The farm at the end.** In this version June keeps it and rents the land to Wade. She could also sell part of it and share the money with Colleen and Teddy. Her choice tells the reader who she has become.
2. **Julian at the end.** The romance can stay finished, or the last pages can leave a small door open.
3. **How dark June becomes in Part Three.** She can accept Lang's bargain with shame, or with a kind of hunger. The second is darker, and the reader will like her less for a while.
4. **The gear lever.** In this version June decides in the last second, when she sees that nothing else will save her. She could also have planned it from the start and kept it from Teddy. That is darker, and closer to revenge.
5. **The place.** Decided: southwest Indiana. A few legal details should still be checked before the court chapters are written.
6. **Where the Tanners are cleared.** Two test readers made Sam their first suspect. He and Ruth need a moment later in the book that clears them.
7. **Ruth and Julian.** Has Ruth signed an option with Julian's company?
8. **Sam after chapter 5.** He has waited at Lang's door (chapter 3) and driven June to the farm (chapter 5). Still open: how often he returns.
9. **The tractor against the fence.** A tractor in gear against a fence for eleven hours would normally stall. Choose before chapter 48: the wheels dig slowly into the wet mud, or a worn clutch slips at idle.
10. **Decided on 17 November: the phone signal.** Chapter 6 says June's phone has no signal at the farmhouse. There is a signal at Ruth Tanner's place and under the power line by the river, and it comes and goes with the day. So on the last night Teddy can telephone the sheriff from the trees by the river field. Somebody should mention the signal by the river before chapter 52.
11. **Decided on 17 November: the night with Julian (chapter 17).** By then June knows the house. She slips out after the chain is on, Julian is waiting for her, and she is back before morning. Mrs. Pruitt does not know, so the calendar shows no missed night.
12. **Who counts June's nights after Mrs. Pruitt dies** (from chapter 33 on)?
13. **Where Julian's rented house stands.** Chapters 1 and 5 say there is nothing on the river road but Ruth's place and Hale Lane.
14. **Who tells June about Lang's fishing cabin** before chapter 32? Lang no longer comes to the farm in chapter 8.
15. **The three quarrels.** The prologue plan says each of the three children quarrelled with Amos in his last week. So far the text has Colleen's two visits (chapter 3) and the threat in the prologue. Wade's quarrel is planned for chapter 8.
16. **The study** (devised on 17 November at Aviel's request; confirm it when chapters 9 to 18 are planned). June does not steal the key. She asks for it, because her glasses are on Amos's desk and she cannot read without them. Mrs. Pruitt gives it to her at once, too easily. The room has been set out for her like a stage: her glasses lie folded on the open diary, and the diary is open at the line written the night before, "The girl came. She is not who she says." Mrs. Pruitt took the glasses so that June would have to go in and read that line. After that the rule is simple: the key lives in Mrs. Pruitt's apron, June must ask for it each time, and so the housekeeper always knows when June is in the study. This also covers chapters 24 and 28, where June sits in the study and searches it.
17. **The weekday of the last night.** June's letters arrive "on Monday", so the last night should fall one to three days before a Monday.
